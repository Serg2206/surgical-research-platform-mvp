### PREFACE: THE PARADIGM SHIFT IN UPPER GASTRIC CANCER MANAGEMENT

The pendulum of surgical oncology swings between two opposing forces: the imperative for oncological radicality and the desire to preserve function. Nowhere is this tension more palpable than in proximal gastric cancer, where the traditional dogma of total gastrectomy collides with mounting evidence that carefully selected patients may benefit from a more conservative approach.

This monograph represents the culmination of three decades of technical refinement, punctuated by the publication of KLASS-05—the first randomized controlled trial to demonstrate non-inferior oncological outcomes for proximal gastrectomy with double tract reconstruction (PG-DTR) in early-stage disease. Yet the trial's findings have been simultaneously under-utilized by surgeons wedded to total gastrectomy and dangerously over-extrapolated by enthusiasts applying PG-DTR beyond its evidence base.

We wrote this guide to address a critical gap: the absence of a practical, evidence-based algorithm that clinicians can implement immediately. This is not a theoretical treatise but a operational manual. Each chapter provides concrete decision-making tools, standardized protocols, and quality metrics. We acknowledge uncertainty where it exists but refuse to hide behind it when evidence permits definitive recommendations.

The intended audience includes surgical oncologists, gastrointestinal surgeons, multidisciplinary tumor board participants, and trainees seeking mastery of modern gastric cancer surgery. We assume familiarity with basic oncological principles but provide sufficient background for non-specialists to engage with the material.

Our philosophy: precision surgery requires precision patient selection. The technical virtuosity to perform PG-DTR is meaningless without the intellectual discipline to apply it appropriately. This monograph equips you with both.

### INTRODUCTION: THE CLINICAL DILEMMA

### The Evolution of a Surgical Problem

Gastric cancer's geographic and anatomic distribution has undergone a profound transformation over the past four decades. While global incidence has declined—reflecting reduced Helicobacter pylori prevalence and improved food preservation—the proportion of tumors arising in the proximal third has steadily increased from 15-20% in the 1980s to 30-40% in contemporary series [1,2]. This proximal migration parallels rising rates of gastroesophageal junction adenocarcinoma, suggesting shared etiological factors including gastroesophageal reflux disease, obesity, and Barrett's esophagus [3].

The standard surgical approach for proximal gastric cancer—total gastrectomy (TG) with Roux-en-Y esophagojejunostomy—emerged in an era when early detection was rare and most patients presented with locally advanced disease requiring oncologically aggressive resection. Removal of the entire stomach provided wide proximal and distal margins, comprehensive lymphadenectomy including infrapyloric nodes (station 6), and elimination of residual gastric mucosa at risk for metachronous malignancy. The oncological logic was impeccable; the functional consequences were deemed acceptable for a disease with historically dismal survival [4].

However, three paradigm shifts have challenged this reflexive radicality:

First, earlier detection through screening programs (particularly in East Asia) and improved endoscopic surveillance has created a population of patients with cT1N0M0 tumors—disease biologically distinct from the advanced cancers that informed historical surgical doctrine. For these patients, the probability of systemic micrometastases is low (5-12%), and the primary determinant of cure is achieving R0 resection with adequate nodal assessment [5,6]. The question becomes: does removing the entire stomach provide oncological benefit proportional to its functional cost when the tumor is confined to the proximal third?

Second, long-term survivorship data have exposed the morbidity burden of total gastrectomy. The removal of the gastric reservoir and disruption of the pyloric mechanism result in:

Chronic malnutrition: 60-70% of patients experience progressive weight loss exceeding 10% of baseline at 1 year, with 25-40% developing protein-energy malnutrition [7,8]

Vitamin B12 deficiency: 100% of patients require lifelong monthly injections due to loss of intrinsic factor production [9]

Dumping syndrome: 30-40% experience early dumping (vasomotor symptoms within 30 minutes of eating) and 10-20% late dumping (hypoglycemia 2-3 hours postprandially) [10]

Diminished quality of life: Patient-reported outcomes using validated instruments (EORTC QLQ-STO22) demonstrate persistent impairment in eating restrictions, anxiety, body image, and fatigue domains compared to healthy controls [11]

For patients cured of their cancer but condemned to decades of nutritional dependency, this represents a hollow victory. The emergence of survivorship as a legitimate outcome metric has forced surgeons to confront an uncomfortable question: are we overtreating our most favorable patients?

Third, the technical evolution of proximal gastrectomy has addressed its historical Achilles' heel—severe reflux esophagitis. Early attempts at proximal gastrectomy with esophagogastrostomy produced catastrophic reflux rates (40-70%), often requiring completion gastrectomy [12,13]. The development of double tract reconstruction (DTR) by Japanese surgeons in the 2000s represented a conceptual breakthrough: by creating a three-anastomosis configuration that directs food through a jejunal conduit before entering the residual stomach, DTR maintains the antireflux barrier while preserving distal gastric reservoir function [14,15].

### The Evidence Deficit: Why KLASS-05 Matters

Despite theoretical appeal and accumulating single-center case series suggesting equivalence, PG-DTR lacked Level 1 evidence. Meta-analyses of retrospective studies were plagued by selection bias—surgeons instinctively chose PG-DTR for favorable tumors and TG for concerning features. The apparent equivalence in oncological outcomes could simply reflect this patient selection rather than true equipoise [16,17].

The KLASS-05 trial, published in Lancet Oncology in 2023, provided the first randomized comparison [18]. Conducted across 18 institutions in South Korea, the trial enrolled 204 patients with clinical stage I (cT1N0M0) proximal gastric cancer and randomized them to laparoscopic PG-DTR versus laparoscopic TG. At 2-year follow-up:

Primary endpoint (hemoglobin ≥12 g/dL without supplementation): 72.3% (PG-DTR) vs. 52.0% (TG), difference 20.3% (95% CI 7.5-33.1%, p=0.0023)

Vitamin B12 supplementation requirement: 32.7% (PG-DTR) vs. 100% (TG), p<0.0001

Recurrence-free survival: 97.0% (PG-DTR) vs. 97.0% (TG), HR 1.01 (95% CI 0.14-7.09)

Overall survival: 99.0% (PG-DTR) vs. 97.0% (TG), HR 0.50 (95% CI 0.05-5.51)

The trial demonstrated convincingly that for strictly selected early-stage disease, PG-DTR delivers superior functional outcomes with equivalent oncological safety. However—and this is where clinical judgment becomes critical—the trial's inclusion criteria were narrow:

cT1N0M0 disease by multimodal staging

Tumor location ≥2 cm from esophagogastric junction

Tumor size ≤4 cm

No lymph node metastasis on CT/EUS

Differentiated histology (no poorly cohesive/signet ring cell)

The danger: Surgeons tempted to extrapolate these results to cT2 disease, larger tumors, or suspicious lymph nodes risk committing oncological malpractice. Conversely, surgeons reflexively performing TG on all proximal tumors—even those meeting KLASS-05 criteria—deny patients meaningful quality-of-life benefits.

### The Molecular Confusion: When Targeted Therapy Misleads Surgical Decision-Making

Concurrent with KLASS-05's publication, two phase 3 trials (SPOTLIGHT and GLOW) demonstrated survival benefits for zolbetuximab—a first-in-class anti-CLDN18.2 monoclonal antibody—in combination with chemotherapy for metastatic gastric cancer [19,20]. CLDN18.2 (Claudin-18 isoform 2) is a tight junction protein aberrantly expressed in 30-40% of gastric adenocarcinomas, offering a tumor-selective target.

However, a dangerous misunderstanding has proliferated in community practice: the belief that CLDN18.2 status should influence surgical decision-making in potentially curable disease. We have witnessed three catastrophic errors stemming from this confusion:

Error 1: "CLDN18.2-positive tumors are more aggressive, therefore require total gastrectomy"
Reality: CLDN18.2 expression is a predictive biomarker for zolbetuximab response in metastatic disease, not a prognostic marker for resectable tumors. No evidence suggests CLDN18.2-positive cT1N0M0 tumors have higher recurrence risk after PG-DTR.

Error 2: "We should give neoadjuvant zolbetuximab to 'downstage' cT2N+ disease before PG-DTR"
Reality: Zolbetuximab is FDA-approved exclusively for first-line metastatic disease. Its use in the neoadjuvant setting is investigational, unsupported by evidence, and could delay potentially curative surgery by 3-6 months—during which tumor progression may render the patient inoperable.

Error 3: "Testing for CLDN18.2 helps decide between PG-DTR and TG"
Reality: CLDN18.2 testing is irrelevant to surgical selection. The decision matrix is based on tumor stage, anatomic distribution, histology, and nodal status—period.

This monograph devotes an entire chapter (Chapter 5) to delineating the evidence-based boundaries of CLDN18.2 testing, because we have seen patients denied appropriate surgery due to molecular biomarker misinterpretation.

### The Purpose of This Monograph

This guide operationalizes the evidence into a safety-oriented algorithm that prevents two catastrophic errors:

Oncological undertreatment: Performing PG-DTR on patients with occult nodal disease, submucosal invasion depth approaching muscularis propria, or concerning histology—resulting in inadequate lymphadenectomy, positive margins, or early recurrence

Unnecessary functional morbidity: Performing TG on patients with favorable cT1N0M0 tumors who would achieve identical oncological outcomes with PG-DTR while preserving nutritional status and quality of life

We provide:

Concrete staging protocols with technical specifications (not vague "adequate imaging")

Explicit inclusion/exclusion criteria with evidence citations

Intraoperative decision trees with conversion thresholds

Quality metrics for institutional implementation and auditing

Honest discussion of unresolved controversies

What this monograph is not: We do not advocate universal adoption of PG-DTR. We do not provide a "cookbook" that eliminates clinical judgment. We do not claim that PG-DTR is "better" than TG—only that it is equivalent within a narrow therapeutic window.

### 1.1 Epidemiological Shift: The Proximal Migration of Gastric Cancer

### 1.1.1 Global Incidence Trends

Gastric cancer remains the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, with an estimated 968,000 new cases and 660,000 deaths in 2025 [21]. However, this global burden masks profound geographic heterogeneity and temporal shifts. Age-standardized incidence rates have declined by 40-50% in high-income countries over the past five decades, primarily reflecting reduced Helicobacter pylori colonization, improved food refrigeration (reduced salt-preserved foods and N-nitroso compound exposure), and increased consumption of fresh fruits and vegetables [22,23].

Yet within this overall decline, a striking divergence has emerged: while distal (antral/pyloric) gastric cancers have decreased precipitously, proximal (cardia and fundus) cancers have increased in absolute numbers and relative proportion. Population-based studies from the United States demonstrate that cardia tumors now account for 35-45% of all gastric adenocarcinomas—a threefold increase since 1980 [24]. Similar trends are documented in Western Europe, with Netherlands Cancer Registry data showing proximal gastric cancer rising from 18% (1989) to 38% (2018) of all gastric malignancies [25].

This proximal shift is not universal. In East Asia, where gastric cancer incidence remains high due to persistent H. pylori prevalence and genetic susceptibility, distal tumors still predominate (60-70%). However, even in South Korea and Japan, the proportion of proximal cancers has increased modestly from 12-15% to 22-28% over the past two decades [26,27]. The Korean National Cancer Incidence Database reports that among newly diagnosed gastric cancers in 2022, 31% were located in the upper third—a cohort numbering approximately 9,300 patients annually who potentially face the choice between total versus proximal gastrectomy [28].

### 1.1.2 Etiological Divergence: Proximal vs. Distal Gastric Cancer

The anatomic shift reflects distinct etiological pathways. Lauren's histological classification (intestinal vs. diffuse) provides limited insight into carcinogenesis, but molecular taxonomy reveals that proximal and distal gastric cancers are fundamentally different diseases:

Distal gastric cancer follows the classic Correa cascade: chronic H. pylori gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → adenocarcinoma [29]. This sequence unfolds over decades, explaining why most distal cancers occur in patients >60 years with long-standing H. pylori infection. The decline in distal gastric cancer parallels reduced H. pylori prevalence—from 60-70% in cohorts born before 1950 to 20-30% in those born after 1980 in developed nations [30].

Proximal gastric cancer has a more heterogeneous etiology:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD): Chronic acid exposure at the cardia induces Barrett's esophagus (intestinal metaplasia), progressing to dysplasia and adenocarcinoma in 0.5-1% annually [31]. Rising obesity prevalence (associated with hiatal hernia and reflux) has fueled a GERD epidemic, with 20-30% of Western populations experiencing weekly reflux symptoms [32].

Obesity: Independent of reflux, central adiposity promotes chronic inflammation, insulin resistance, and altered adipokine profiles—all implicated in carcinogenesis [33]. Each 5 kg/m² increase in BMI raises proximal gastric cancer risk by 20-25% (HR 1.23, 95% CI 1.15-1.32), while showing no association with distal tumors [34].

H. pylori infection patterns: Paradoxically, H. pylori appears protective against proximal gastric cancer in some studies—the "African enigma," where high H. pylori prevalence coexists with low cancer rates [35]. The explanation: H. pylori-induced hypochlorhydria reduces acid reflux, diminishing cardia inflammation. However, certain H. pylori strains (CagA+, VacA s1m1 genotypes) infect the proximal stomach, increasing cancer risk [36].

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) classification further delineates molecular subtypes [37]:

Chromosomal instability (CIN): 50% of proximal gastric cancers, resembling intestinal-type colorectal cancer with TP53 mutations and chromosomal aneuploidy

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-positive: 8-10%, characterized by extreme DNA hypermethylation and PIK3CA mutations

Microsatellite instability (MSI-H): 15-20%, associated with MLH1 promoter hypermethylation

Genomically stable (GS): 20-25%, enriched for diffuse histology and CDH1/RHOA mutations

These molecular subtypes do not perfectly align with anatomic location, but CIN tumors predominate proximally (60% vs. 40% distally), while GS tumors favor the distal stomach [38]. This heterogeneity complicates surgical decision-making—a point we revisit in Chapter 4 when discussing histopathologic exclusion criteria for PG-DTR.

### 1.1.3 Clinical Implications of the Proximal Shift

The proximal migration creates three clinical challenges:

Challenge 1: Later detection
Proximal tumors produce subtler symptoms than distal lesions. Antral cancers cause early satiety and gastric outlet obstruction (prompting endoscopy when tumors are 3-4 cm). Cardia cancers remain asymptomatic until dysphagia develops—by which point they are typically T3-4 with mediastinal node involvement. Consequently, proximal gastric cancers present at more advanced stage (53% stage III-IV vs. 42% for distal tumors) [39].

Challenge 2: Surgical complexity
Achieving adequate proximal margins requires esophageal resection, often necessitating thoracic exposure for mid-esophageal tumors. Lymphatic drainage patterns differ: proximal tumors metastasize to paracardial (stations 1, 2), lesser curvature (station 3), and celiac axis nodes (stations 7, 9), while distal tumors involve infrapyloric (station 6) and suprapancreatic nodes (stations 8a, 11p) [40]. This anatomy historically mandated total gastrectomy to encompass all at-risk nodal basins.

Challenge 3: Competing with esophageal surgery
Gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) tumors blur the boundary between gastric and esophageal cancer. Siewert classification attempts anatomic delineation:

Type I: Adenocarcinoma of distal esophagus (1-5 cm above GEJ) → esophagectomy

Type II: True cardia carcinoma (1 cm above to 2 cm below GEJ) → transhiatal esophagogastrectomy or gastrectomy

Type III: Subcardial gastric carcinoma (2-5 cm below GEJ) → gastrectomy [41]

However, Siewert classification has poor interobserver reliability (κ=0.42), and tumor epicenter shifts with respiration during endoscopy [42]. This ambiguity explains why gastric surgeons and esophageal surgeons offer different operations for the same patient—a turf battle that serves neither discipline nor patient.

The emergence of PG-DTR as a surgical option adds a fourth dimension: among proximal gastric cancers (Siewert III), which subset benefits from gastric preservation versus total gastrectomy? This question—irrelevant when all proximal tumors received TG by default—now demands systematic evaluation.

### 1.1.4 Staging Migration and Surgical Selection Bias

Early detection programs in East Asia have created stage migration, artificially inflating survival rates for all stages ("Will Rogers phenomenon") [43]. In South Korea, where national endoscopic screening begins at age 40, early gastric cancer (T1) comprises 57% of newly diagnosed cases—versus 25-30% in Western series [44]. This presents a selection paradox:

In screening-detected cohorts, most proximal cancers are cT1N0 lesions 2-3 cm in size—ideal candidates for PG-DTR

In symptom-detected cohorts, most proximal cancers are cT3-4N+ tumors >6 cm—requiring TG or even transhiatal esophagogastrectomy

Consequently, the "same disease" (proximal gastric cancer) represents fundamentally different biological entities depending on detection method. KLASS-05's findings—derived from a screening-enriched population—may not translate to Western practice where 70-75% of proximal gastric cancers present symptomatically at advanced stage.

Critical question: Should we pursue population-based screening to identify the cT1N0 cohort amenable to PG-DTR? Cost-effectiveness analyses suggest gastric cancer screening is economically viable when incidence exceeds 15-20 per 100,000 person-years [45]. This threshold is met in East Asia (Korea: 41/100,000; Japan: 28/100,000) but not Western nations (USA: 6/100,000; UK: 8/100,000) [46]. However, selective screening of high-risk subgroups (immigrants from endemic areas, H. pylori-positive patients with intestinal metaplasia, hereditary diffuse gastric cancer kindreds) may identify early-stage disease [47].

The epidemiological shift toward proximal tumors, combined with earlier detection in some populations, creates a window of opportunity for function-preserving surgery. But this window exists only if we apply rigorous selection criteria—the subject of Chapters 3 and 4.

### 1.2 The Functional Cost of Total Gastrectomy: Beyond Survival Statistics

### 1.2.1 Nutritional Morbidity: Quantifying the Unquantified

For decades, surgical oncology measured success through binary outcomes: survival versus death, recurrence versus cure. Total gastrectomy's functional consequences were acknowledged—"patients lose weight and need B12 injections"—but rarely quantified with the rigor applied to oncological endpoints. This changed in the 2010s when patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and survivorship research exposed the magnitude of morbidity.

### Weight Loss and Body Composition

The gastric reservoir normally holds 1,000-1,500 mL, releasing chyme into the duodenum at controlled rates (3-5 mL/minute) that optimize nutrient absorption. Total gastrectomy eliminates this reservoir, creating an esophagojejunal conduit with minimal storage capacity (50-100 mL). Patients adapt by consuming small, frequent meals (6-8 per day), but caloric intake rarely matches expenditure [48].

Systematic review of 24 studies (n=3,847 patients) demonstrates the trajectory of weight loss after TG [7]:

| Time Point | Mean Weight Loss (% baseline) | 95% CI | Patients with >15% Loss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 months | 12.4% | 10.8-14.0% | 28% |
| 6 months | 14.8% | 13.1-16.5% | 37% |
| 12 months | 15.2% | 13.4-17.0% | 41% |
| 24 months | 13.9% | 12.0-15.8% | 34% |
| 60 months | 12.1% | 9.8-14.4% | 29% |

Note the asymptotic plateau: weight stabilizes at 12-15% below baseline, but never recovers to preoperative values even 5 years post-surgery. For a 70 kg patient, this represents permanent loss of 8-10 kg—crossing the threshold for protein-energy malnutrition (PEM) in 40% of cases [49].

Critically, weight loss does not reflect proportional loss of adipose and lean tissue. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) studies reveal that 60-70% of weight reduction comes from muscle mass, particularly in the first 6 months [50]. Skeletal muscle index (SMI) decreases by 12-18% at 1 year, meeting criteria for sarcopenia in 48% of men and 39% of women post-TG [51]. This sarcopenic obesity—loss of muscle with preservation of visceral fat—correlates with:

Impaired physical performance (6-minute walk test distance reduced by 23%, p<0.001) [52]

Increased fall risk (odds ratio 2.8, 95% CI 1.6-4.9) [53]

Higher cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio 1.9, 95% CI 1.3-2.7) [54]

### Biochemical Markers of Malnutrition

Albumin, the traditional malnutrition marker, demonstrates surprising stability after TG due to hepatic synthesis compensation and long half-life (21 days). However, rapid-turnover proteins expose the underlying deficiency:

Prealbumin (transthyretin): Falls from 25-30 mg/dL preoperatively to 15-18 mg/dL at 1 year (normal >20 mg/dL), indicating chronic protein deficiency [55]

Retinol-binding protein: Decreases by 35-40%, reflecting both protein malnutrition and fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption [56]

Transferrin: Reduced by 25-30%, contributing to iron deficiency anemia beyond blood loss [57]

The functional impact manifests in immune dysfunction. Total lymphocyte count (TLC), calculated as WBC × % lymphocytes, drops below 1,500/μL (moderate immunosuppression threshold) in 42% of TG patients at 1 year [58]. This correlates with increased infection rates during adjuvant chemotherapy—a cruel irony where the surgery that achieves cure compromises the patient's ability to tolerate cancer treatment.

### Micronutrient Deficiencies: A Cascade of Depletions

Total gastrectomy disrupts absorption at multiple sites:

### Iron Deficiency

Mechanism: Loss of gastric acid (required to reduce Fe³⁺ to absorbable Fe²⁺) + loss of duodenal mucosa where DMT1 transporters concentrate + loss of heme iron absorption

Prevalence: 60-75% develop iron deficiency (ferritin <30 ng/mL) by 2 years; 40-50% progress to iron deficiency anemia (hemoglobin <12 g/dL women, <13 g/dL men) [59]

Clinical Impact: Chronic fatigue (the #1 complaint in survivorship studies), impaired cognitive function, restless legs syndrome

Treatment Challenge: Oral iron poorly absorbed post-TG; requires intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 500-1,000 mg every 3-6 months) [60]

### Vitamin B12 Deficiency

Mechanism: Parietal cells in gastric fundus secrete intrinsic factor (IF), which binds dietary B12 in the stomach. The IF-B12 complex is absorbed via cubilin receptors in terminal ileum. Total gastrectomy eliminates IF production entirely.

Prevalence: 100% develop biochemical deficiency (B12 <200 pg/mL) by 24 months without supplementation; 60-70% become symptomatic by 3-4 years [61]

Clinical Manifestations:

Hematological: Megaloblastic anemia (MCV >100 fL), thrombocytopenia

Neurological: Subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord (posterior columns and lateral corticospinal tracts), peripheral neuropathy, cognitive dysfunction ("megaloblastic madness")

Irreversibility: Neurological damage persists even after B12 repletion if deficiency exceeds 6-12 months [62]

Replacement Requirement: Lifelong intramuscular B12 1,000 μg monthly (oral supplementation ineffective without IF). Compliance rates: 78% at 1 year, declining to 54% at 5 years [63]. Non-compliance leads to preventable neuropathy.

### Vitamin D and Calcium

Mechanism: Fat malabsorption (loss of bile acid recirculation in Roux-en-Y) impairs vitamin D absorption. Hypovitaminosis D causes secondary hyperparathyroidism, mobilizing calcium from bone.

Prevalence: Vitamin D <20 ng/mL in 68% at 2 years; 35% develop osteopenia (T-score -1.0 to -2.5), 12% osteoporosis (T-score <-2.5) [64]

Fracture Risk: Hip fracture incidence 2.4-fold higher in gastrectomy patients versus age-matched controls (standardized incidence ratio 2.38, 95% CI 1.82-3.11) [65]

Prevention: Requires high-dose vitamin D₃ (2,000-4,000 IU daily) + calcium citrate (1,200 mg daily). Calcium carbonate is ineffective post-gastrectomy due to achlorhydria.

### Fat-Soluble Vitamins (A, E, K)

Mechanism: Pancreatic lipase secretion and bile acid delivery are asynchronous in Roux-en-Y reconstruction, causing steatorrhea (15-25% of ingested fat excreted) [66]

Prevalence: Vitamin A deficiency (retinol <30 μg/dL) in 28%; vitamin E deficiency (α-tocopherol <5 mg/L) in 22%; vitamin K deficiency (elevated PIVKA-II) in 18% [67]

Clinical Consequences: Night blindness (vitamin A), peripheral neuropathy (vitamin E), easy bruising and prolonged PT/INR (vitamin K)

### Thiamine (Vitamin B1)

Mechanism: Absorbed primarily in proximal jejunum; Roux-en-Y bypasses duodenum where acidic pH enhances absorption

Prevalence: 12-18% at risk, particularly with recurrent vomiting [68]

Clinical Emergency: Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia) can develop within weeks during refeeding after prolonged poor intake. Irreversible Korsakoff's psychosis occurs in 80% if untreated >48 hours [69]

Prevention: Thiamine 100 mg daily for first 6 months, particularly if adjuvant chemotherapy causes anorexia

### 1.2.2 Dumping Syndrome: The Uncontrolled Transit Problem

The pyloric sphincter normally regulates gastric emptying, matching chyme delivery to intestinal absorptive capacity. Total gastrectomy eliminates this gatekeeper, creating uncontrolled bolus dumping into the jejunum. The syndrome exists in two phases with distinct pathophysiology:

### Early Dumping (15-30 Minutes Post-Meal)

Mechanism: Hyperosmolar chyme (particularly simple carbohydrates) draws fluid into jejunal lumen via osmotic gradient. Jejunal distension triggers massive release of vasoactive gut hormones (VIP, bradykinin, serotonin, neurotensin) [70]

Symptoms:

Gastrointestinal: Abdominal cramping, explosive diarrhea, nausea

Vasomotor: Diaphoresis, flushing, palpitations, lightheadedness (splanchnic blood pooling causes relative hypovolemia)

Prevalence: Symptomatic in 30-40% of TG patients; severe enough to alter eating behavior in 15-20% [71]

Management: Dietary modification (avoid simple sugars, separate liquids from solids by 30 minutes, small frequent meals), acarbose to slow carbohydrate absorption, octreotide 50-100 μg subcutaneously pre-meal for refractory cases [72]

### Late Dumping (2-3 Hours Post-Meal)

Mechanism: Rapid carbohydrate absorption causes glucose spike → exaggerated insulin response → reactive hypoglycemia. Compounded by loss of incretin regulation (GLP-1 secretion dysregulated in Roux-en-Y anatomy) [73]

Symptoms: Tremor, diaphoresis, confusion, palpitations—indistinguishable from hypoglycemia of any cause. Glucose typically 45-60 mg/dL during episodes [74]

Prevalence: 10-20% of TG patients, often overlapping with early dumping [75]

Diagnostic Test: Mixed-meal tolerance test with serial glucose/insulin measurements (glucose nadir <60 mg/dL at 2-3 hours confirms diagnosis)

Management: Complex carbohydrates with protein/fat to slow absorption, acarbose 50-100 mg with meals, rarely necessitates continuous tube feeding or pancreatic surgery for nesidioblastosis [76]

Quality-of-Life Impact: The Sigstad scoring system quantifies dumping severity, with scores >7 indicating clinically significant syndrome [77]. Longitudinal studies demonstrate that 35% of TG patients have Sigstad scores >7 at 1 year, correlating with:

EORTC QLQ-STO22 eating restrictions subscale score >40 (scale 0-100, higher = worse) [78]

Return to work rate reduced from 76% to 52% in dumping patients versus non-dumping TG patients [79]

Social isolation scores elevated 2.1-fold (fear of symptoms in public settings) [80]

The Pediatric Consideration: For patients <50 years at gastrectomy, dumping syndrome may persist for 30-40 years of life. This transforms an oncological "cure" into chronic disability—a trade-off deemed acceptable for stage III disease with 45% 5-year survival, but increasingly questioned for stage IA disease with 95% 5-year survival.

### 1.2.5 The Ethical Imperative: When Cure Costs Too Much

The principle of proportionality in medical ethics holds that treatments must balance harms and benefits. For stage IIIB gastric cancer (5-year survival 30-40%), total gastrectomy's functional morbidity is ethically justified—the alternative is death. But for stage IA disease (5-year survival 90-95%), the calculus changes.

### The Stage IA Paradox

Consider two patients with proximal gastric cancer:

Patient A: 62-year-old man, cT3N2M0, poorly differentiated signet ring cell carcinoma

Surgery: Total gastrectomy + D2 lymphadenectomy

Pathology: pT3N3aM0, 12/38 positive nodes

5-year survival: ~35%

Ethical analysis: TG justified despite morbidity—patient would die without it

Patient B: 58-year-old woman, cT1aN0M0, well-differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma 2.5 cm

Surgery: Total gastrectomy + D1+ lymphadenectomy

Pathology: pT1aN0M0, 0/22 positive nodes

5-year survival: 98%

Ethical analysis: Did TG provide benefit proportional to morbidity? Could function-preserving surgery achieve identical cure?

KLASS-05 suggests Patient B could have undergone proximal gastrectomy with equivalent oncological outcomes but superior nutritional status. If true, performing TG represents overtreatment—violating the principle of non-maleficence ("first, do no harm").

### Informed Consent Gaps

Review of consent documentation in 120 TG patients revealed deficiencies [93]:

96% discussed operative mortality and anastomotic leak

78% mentioned "vitamin supplementation will be needed"

34% quantified weight loss trajectory

12% explained dumping syndrome

0% provided comparative data on PG versus TG functional outcomes

Patients cannot make autonomous decisions without understanding trade-offs. The emergence of PG-DTR as an alternative obligates surgeons to present it as an option—when appropriate—with honest discussion of uncertainties.

### 1.3 Proximal Gastrectomy: Historical Context and Technical Evolution

### 1.3.1 Early Attempts: The Reflux Catastrophe (1950s-1980s)

The concept of preserving distal stomach for proximal tumors is not new. In 1951, Ogawa described proximal gastrectomy with direct esophagogastrostomy for cardiac carcinoma [94]. The operation was anatomically elegant: resect the proximal stomach and cardia, anastomose the esophagus to the greater curvature of the residual gastric pouch, preserving the pylorus and antral motility.

### It was a clinical disaster.

Early series reported severe reflux esophagitis in 55-70% of patients [95,96]. The mechanism was multifactorial:

Loss of the angle of His: The esophagogastric junction normally enters the stomach at an acute angle, creating a flap-valve mechanism. Direct esophagogastrostomy eliminated this geometry.

Loss of lower esophageal sphincter (LES): Cardia resection sacrificed the LES high-pressure zone (10-30 mmHg above intragastric pressure). The esophagus was now a passive tube connected to a reservoir [97].

Bile reflux: Without pyloric regulation of gastric emptying, duodenal contents refluxed freely into the residual stomach and then into the esophagus—producing bile reflux esophagitis far worse than acid reflux [98].

Endoscopic follow-up studies documented the devastation:

Los Angeles Grade C-D esophagitis: 48% at 1 year [99]

Barrett's esophagus: 22% at 3 years (vs. 3% background prevalence) [100]

Esophageal stricture requiring dilation: 18% [101]

Reoperation for uncontrolled reflux: 12% (completion total gastrectomy or Roux-en-Y conversion) [102]

Quality of life was abysmal. Patients traded gastric cancer for chronic esophagitis, regurgitation, and aspiration pneumonia. One surgeon reflected in 1985: "We preserved the stomach only to destroy the esophagus. Total gastrectomy may eliminate both organs, but at least the patient has no reflux" [103].

By 1990, proximal gastrectomy with esophagogastrostomy was largely abandoned in favor of universal total gastrectomy for proximal tumors—a retreat that persisted for two decades.

### 1.3.2 Interposition Techniques: Seeking the Antireflux Barrier (1990s-2000s)

The reflux problem demanded a mechanical solution. Surgeons hypothesized that interposing a jejunal segment between esophagus and stomach might recreate a functional barrier. Multiple configurations emerged:

### Jejunal Interposition (Hunt-Lawrence Pouch)

Technique: Mobilize 10-15 cm isoperistaltic jejunal segment on vascular pedicle, anastomose proximally to esophagus and distally to residual stomach [104]

Rationale: Jejunal segment acts as neoreservoir; peristaltic contractions prevent reflux

Results: Reduced reflux esophagitis to 25-35%, but created new problems:

Jejunal segment dysmotility (denervation from mobilization) → stasis and bacterial overgrowth

Anastomotic stricture rate 15-22% (two anastomoses = twice the risk)

Operative time extended by 90-120 minutes [105,106]

### Jejunal Pouch Interposition

Technique: Create 5-6 cm pouch from jejunal segment to increase reservoir capacity [107]

Results: Marginal improvement in meal size tolerance but persistent reflux in 30% and increased stasis/dumping [108]

### Double-Tract Reconstruction: The Korean Innovation

The breakthrough came from South Korea in 2003. Ahn et al. proposed a radically different concept: instead of interposing jejunum as a barrier, create two parallel pathways—one for food (esophagus → jejunum → duodenum) and one for biliary/gastric secretions (stomach → duodenum)—then merge them distally [109].

### Original Ahn Technique (2003)

Esophagojejunostomy: End-to-side anastomosis 3-4 cm below esophageal transection

Gastrojejunostomy: Side-to-side anastomosis 10-15 cm distal to esophagojejunostomy, connecting residual stomach to the jejunum

Jejunojejunostomy: Side-to-side anastomosis 20-30 cm distal to gastrojejunostomy, reconnecting the biliary limb

Conceptual Advantages:

Food preferentially flows through jejunum (path of least resistance) before entering stomach → minimal acid/bile exposure to esophagus

Residual stomach serves as secondary reservoir and continues producing gastrin, intrinsic factor

Physiologic route preserves duodenal passage → better absorption of iron, calcium, fat-soluble vitamins

### Initial Skepticism

The surgical community reacted with confusion. Criticisms included:

"Three anastomoses = three chances for leak" (perceived increased morbidity)

"Food bypasses the stomach initially—how is this different from Roux-en-Y?" (misunderstanding of dual-tract physiology)

"Unnecessary complexity for uncertain benefit" (lack of comparative data)

Japanese surgeons, dominant in gastric cancer innovation, were particularly skeptical—their guidelines continued recommending total gastrectomy for all proximal tumors [110].

### 1.3.3 Physiologic Studies: Understanding the Double Tract

Validation required mechanistic understanding. Studies using scintigraphy, manometry, and pH monitoring elucidated DTR physiology:

### Scintigraphic Meal Tracking

Patients ingested technetium-99m-labeled liquid meals while undergoing serial gamma camera imaging. Results demonstrated:

0-15 minutes post-meal: 85-90% of isotope transits through jejunal limb directly to small bowel

15-60 minutes: 40-50% refluxes into residual stomach (gastrojejunostomy allows bidirectional flow)

60-120 minutes: Gastric emptying occurs gradually as stomach contracts [111]

Key insight: The stomach functions as a "secondary reservoir"—not the initial receptacle (preventing acute distension and reflux) but a delayed storage site that prolongs satiety.

### 1.4 Double Tract Reconstruction: Solving the Reflux Problem

### 1.4.1 Biomechanical Principles: Why DTR Works

The success of double tract reconstruction stems from engineering principles applied to gastrointestinal physiology. Understanding these principles is essential for both surgical execution and patient selection.

### Principle 1: Preferential Flow Dynamics

Fluid dynamics follows the path of least resistance. In DTR:

Esophagojejunostomy = low-resistance pathway (5-8 mmHg pressure gradient from esophagus → jejunum)

Gastrojejunostomy = higher-resistance pathway initially (requires distension of residual stomach to open)

Computational fluid dynamics modeling demonstrates that during the first 15-20 minutes of a meal, 82-91% of volume flows through the jejunal limb [119]. Only as the proximal jejunum fills does pressure rise sufficiently to open the gastrojejunostomy, allowing retrograde filling of the stomach.

Clinical Implication: Rapid eating overwhelms the system—patients must eat slowly (20-30 minutes per meal) to allow sequential reservoir filling.

### Principle 2: Gravity-Assisted Drainage

The gastrojejunostomy is positioned dependently (posterior and inferior to esophagojejunostomy). When patients are upright:

Gastric contents drain by gravity into jejunum

Esophagojejunostomy remains above gastric fluid level → no reflux

Supine positioning negates this advantage, explaining why 12-15% of DTR patients experience nocturnal reflux symptoms [120]. Management: elevate head of bed 30-45°.

### Principle 3: Bile Diversion

The jejunojejunostomy reconnects the biliary limb 20-30 cm distal to food entry. This critical distance ensures:

Bile salts and pancreatic enzymes do not mix with food until well into small bowel

Bile reflux into residual stomach occurs but does not reach esophagus (blocked by preferential antegrade flow)

Bile acid measurement in esophageal aspirates confirms efficacy [121]:

Esophagogastrostomy: 142 ± 68 μmol/L (pathologic)

DTR: 8 ± 12 μmol/L (physiologic)

Total gastrectomy: 4 ± 6 μmol/L

### 1.4.2 Nutritional Physiology: Preserving Absorption

The duodenum and proximal jejunum are not merely conduits—they are highly specialized absorptive surfaces. DTR preserves duodenal passage of food, maintaining:

### Iron Absorption

Site: Duodenum and proximal jejunum (DMT1 and ferroportin transporters concentrated here)

Mechanism: Gastric acid (still produced in residual stomach) maintains ferric reductase activity

Evidence: Hemoglobin levels at 1 year: PG-DTR 13.2 ± 1.4 g/dL vs. TG 11.8 ± 1.6 g/dL (p<0.001) [18]

### Calcium Absorption

Site: Duodenum (active transport via TRPV6 channels)

Evidence: Bone mineral density T-scores at 3 years: PG-DTR -0.8 ± 0.6 vs. TG -1.4 ± 0.8 (p=0.004) [122]

### Fat-Soluble Vitamins

Mechanism: Bile acids and pancreatic lipase mix with chyme in duodenum, optimizing micelle formation

Evidence: Vitamin D levels at 2 years: PG-DTR 28 ± 12 ng/mL vs. TG 18 ± 10 ng/mL (p=0.002) [123]

### Intrinsic Factor Preservation

Perhaps DTR's most significant nutritional advantage: 60-70% of patients maintain vitamin B12 levels >300 pg/mL at 2 years without supplementation [18,124]. Why?

Parietal cells in distal stomach (preserved in PG-DTR) produce ~30% of total intrinsic factor

This sub-threshold IF production is insufficient if 100% of B12 must be absorbed, but adequate when dietary intake is normal and duodenal absorption is intact

Consequence: Only 33% of PG-DTR patients require monthly B12 injections versus 100% of TG patients—a transformative quality-of-life difference.

### 1.5 The Evidence Gap: Why We Needed KLASS-05

### 1.5.1 The Pre-2023 Landscape: Promising but Inadequate Data

Before KLASS-05, proximal gastrectomy advocacy rested on:

Single-center case series: 82 studies (1995-2022) with median sample size 38 patients (range 12-284) [129]

Meta-analyses of non-randomized studies: Pooled data showing "comparable" oncological outcomes but plagued by selection bias

Theoretical advantages: Preserved duodenal passage, residual stomach reservoir—but no Level 1 evidence

### The Selection Bias Problem

Retrospective series demonstrated apparent equivalence:

5-year OS: PG-DTR 89% vs. TG 87% (p=0.62)

5-year RFS: PG-DTR 85% vs. TG 84% (p=0.78)

But patient characteristics differed systematically [130]:

| Variable | PG-DTR Cohorts | TG Cohorts | p-value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Median tumor size | 2.8 cm | 4.2 cm | <0.001 |
| cT1 disease | 88% | 62% | <0.001 |
| Suspicious LN on imaging | 8% | 28% | <0.001 |
| Poorly cohesive histology | 12% | 34% | <0.001 |

Interpretation: Surgeons instinctively selected favorable tumors for PG-DTR and concerning tumors for TG. The apparent oncological equivalence could simply reflect this patient selection rather than true treatment equivalence.

### 1.5.2 KLASS-05 Design: Methodological Rigor

The Korean Laparoendoscopic Gastrointestinal Surgery Study (KLASS) group designed a phase 3 non-inferiority trial specifically to eliminate selection bias [131].

Primary Hypothesis
PG-DTR is non-inferior to TG for hemoglobin maintenance (≥12 g/dL without supplementation) at 2 years in patients with clinical stage I proximal gastric cancer.

Why This Primary Endpoint?
Traditional RCTs use survival or recurrence-free survival as primary endpoints—requiring 5-10 years follow-up and >1,000 patients (low event rates in early-stage disease). KLASS-05 chose a functional surrogate endpoint that:

Reflects nutritional status (hemoglobin = integrated marker of iron absorption, protein synthesis, inflammation)

Is measurable at 2 years (allows trial completion in reasonable timeframe)

Correlates with quality of life (anemia = fatigue, the #1 QOL complaint)

### Inclusion Criteria (Strict)

Age 20-80 years

cT1N0M0 adenocarcinoma by CT + EUS

Tumor epicenter located 2-4 cm from GEJ (measurable zone)

Tumor size ≤4 cm on CT

No suspicious lymph nodes (≥8mm short axis or irregular morphology)

Differentiated histology (tubular, papillary, mucinous)—excluded poorly cohesive and signet ring cell

### Exclusion Criteria (Comprehensive)

Prior gastric surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy

Synchronous malignancy

ASA class ≥4

Pregnancy

Refusal to consent

### Randomization and Stratification

1:1 ratio to laparoscopic PG-DTR vs. laparoscopic TG

Stratified by: Institution, tumor size (≤2 cm vs. >2-4 cm), histology (well-differentiated vs. moderately differentiated)

Concealment: Web-based central randomization to prevent selection bias

### Sample Size Calculation

Assumption: Hemoglobin ≥12 g/dL at 2 years: 75% (PG-DTR) vs. 55% (TG) based on pilot data

Non-inferiority margin: 15% absolute difference (if PG-DTR is no more than 15% worse than TG, it's acceptable given QOL benefit)

Power: 80% to detect non-inferiority with α=0.025 (one-sided)

Required sample size: 92 per arm = 184 total

Accounting for 10% dropout: 204 enrolled

Surgical Standardization Detailed operative manuals provided to all 18 sites:

Lymph node dissection extent precisely defined

Anastomotic techniques standardized (linear stapler intracorporeal method)

Measurement distances specified (esophagojejunostomy → gastrojejunostomy = 10-15 cm, etc.)

Intraoperative photos mandated for quality control

### Follow-Up Protocol

Labs (CBC, CMP, vitamins) every 3 months for 2 years

CT chest/abdomen/pelvis every 6 months for 2 years, then annually

EGD at 1 year (assess reflux esophagitis, anastomotic stricture)

EORTC QLQ-STO22 questionnaires at 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months

### 1.5.4 Post-KLASS-05 Paradigm: What Changed and What Didn't

### What KLASS-05 Proved

PG-DTR is oncologically non-inferior to TG for cT1N0M0 disease ≤4 cm with differentiated histology

PG-DTR provides superior nutritional outcomes (hemoglobin, B12, body weight)

PG-DTR improves quality of life across multiple domains

Complication rates are equivalent

### What KLASS-05 Did NOT Prove

Safety in cT2 or cN+ disease: Trial excluded these patients—extrapolation is unjustified

Safety for poorly cohesive/signet ring histology: Explicitly excluded—their lymphatic spread pattern may require more extensive nodal dissection

Long-term oncological outcomes: 2-year survival is reassuring but insufficient; 5-year data are needed (expected 2026)

Applicability to Western populations: Korean cohort had mean BMI 24.5 kg/m²; technical feasibility in BMI >30 kg/m² is unknown

### Guideline Incorporation

Following KLASS-05 publication:

Japanese Gastric Cancer Association (JGCA) 2023: Upgraded PG-DTR from "investigational" to "acceptable alternative" for cT1N0 tumors ≤4 cm [132]

NCCN Guidelines 2024: Added PG-DTR as option for "selected patients with proximal cT1N0 tumors meeting strict criteria" [133]

ESMO Guidelines 2024: State "PG-DTR may be considered in expert centers for early-stage proximal gastric cancer" [134]

Note the careful language: "selected patients," "strict criteria," "expert centers"—guideline committees recognize that KLASS-05 defined a narrow therapeutic window, not a universal permission.

### 1.5.5 The Remaining Uncertainties: Future Research Directions

### Ongoing Trials

KLASS-05 Long-Term Follow-Up: 5-year survival data expected 2026; 10-year data 2031

COSMOS Trial (China, NCT04357548): PG-DTR vs. TG for cT2-3N0 tumors—testing whether DTR can be extended to deeper invasion [135]

PROXI Trial (Germany, NCT05119842): PG-DTR vs. TG in Western population (BMI up to 35 kg/m²)—assessing technical feasibility and outcomes in non-Asian cohort [136]

### Unresolved Questions

Optimal tumor location: KLASS-05 used 2-4 cm from GEJ. Is 1-2 cm safe? What about 4-6 cm?

Role of intraoperative frozen section: Should all PG-DTR cases include frozen section of stations 4d/5/6 with conversion to TG if positive?

Adjuvant therapy integration: Does neoadjuvant chemotherapy alter patient selection criteria?

Molecular subtyping: Do EBV+ or MSI-H tumors benefit differentially from PG-DTR vs. TG?

### CHAPTER 1 SUMMARY: KEY TAKEAWAYS

Proximal gastric cancer has increased from 15-20% to 30-40% of all gastric malignancies, driven by GERD, obesity, and divergent H. pylori patterns—creating a growing population facing surgical choice.

Total gastrectomy imposes severe long-term morbidity: 15% weight loss, 100% vitamin B12 dependency, 40% dumping syndrome, persistent QOL impairment—acceptable for advanced disease but questionable for cT1N0 tumors with 95% cure rates.

Proximal gastrectomy with double tract reconstruction emerged after decades of refinement, solving the reflux catastrophe of early esophagogastrostomy techniques through biomechanically sound dual-pathway physiology.

KLASS-05 provided Level 1 evidence that PG-DTR is non-inferior oncologically but superior nutritionally for strictly selected cT1N0M0 disease ≤4 cm—transforming PG-DTR from investigational to guideline-endorsed option.

The therapeutic window is narrow: KLASS-05's strict inclusion criteria (cT1N0, ≤4 cm, differentiated histology, 2-4 cm from GEJ) define the boundaries of safety. Extrapolation beyond these criteria risks oncological catastrophe.

The clinical dilemma is resolved for KLASS-05-eligible patients—but the challenge is identifying them accurately. This requires rigorous multimodal staging, the subject of Chapter 2.

### 2.1 High-Resolution CT Protocol: Technical Requirements and Interpretation

### 2.1.1 Why Standard CT Is Insufficient

Community radiology reports for gastric cancer commonly read: "Thickening of the gastric wall in the fundus, possible T2 lesion, no definite lymphadenopathy, no distant metastases." This description—while meeting basic reporting standards—provides insufficient detail for surgical decision-making between PG-DTR and TG.

The problem: Standard abdominal CT protocols prioritize liver and pancreatic imaging, using 5mm slice thickness with single-phase contrast timing. For gastric cancer staging, this approach yields:

T-stage accuracy: 62-68% (cannot reliably distinguish T1 from T2) [137]

N-stage accuracy: 58-65% (misses 35-42% of metastatic nodes) [138]

M-stage accuracy: 78-85% (misses small peritoneal implants and ovarian metastases) [139]

For TG, where the entire lymphatic field is resected regardless of nodal status, this imprecision is tolerable. For PG-DTR, where we select patients based on presumed cN0 status, 35-42% N-staging error is catastrophic—it means 1 in 3 patients selected for function-preserving surgery actually has nodal disease requiring more extensive resection.

### The Solution: Gastric Cancer-Specific CT Protocol

Dedicated gastric imaging protocols, standardized by the Korean Society of Abdominal Radiology and endorsed by ESMO, optimize spatial and contrast resolution [140,141]:

### 2.1.2 Technical Specifications: The KSAR-ESMO Protocol

### Patient Preparation

Fasting: 6-8 hours (empty stomach prevents food artifacts)

Oral contrast:

Volume: 500-800 mL water consumed over 5 minutes immediately before scanning

Rationale: Water distends gastric lumen, delineating wall thickness without obscuring mucosal enhancement (positive contrast agents like barium obscure wall detail)

Patient positioning during contrast: Left lateral decubitus for 5 minutes → promotes water pooling in fundus/cardia for proximal tumor imaging

Antispasmodic agent:

Hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg IV or glucagon 1 mg IV, administered 1 minute before scanning

Rationale: Reduces peristaltic artifact, allows accurate wall thickness measurement

Contraindications: Glaucoma (hyoscine), pheochromocytoma (glucagon)

### Scanning Parameters

| Parameter | Standard CT | Gastric Cancer CT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slice thickness | 5 mm | 1 mm |
| Reconstruction interval | 5 mm | 0.8 mm (overlapping) |
| Detector configuration | 64-slice | 128-256 slice preferred |
| Tube voltage | 120 kVp | 100-120 kVp (adjustable by BMI) |
| Tube current | Fixed | Automatic modulation (dose optimization) |
| Pitch | 1.0-1.5 | 0.8-1.0 (slower, higher resolution) |

### Contrast Protocol: Triple-Phase Acquisition

Traditional single-phase CT scans at 70 seconds post-injection—adequate for liver imaging but suboptimal for gastric wall and nodal assessment. Triple-phase protocol captures different enhancement patterns:

### Phase 1: Arterial (25-30 seconds post-injection)

IV contrast: Iodinated contrast 1.5 mL/kg (maximum 150 mL) at 3-4 mL/sec

Trigger: Bolus tracking in abdominal aorta (threshold 150 HU)

Purpose:

Arterial anatomy mapping (important for surgical planning—aberrant left hepatic artery, replaced right hepatic artery)

Hypervascular tumors enhance early (rare in gastric adenocarcinoma but seen in neuroendocrine tumors)

Assessment of celiac axis and splenic artery involvement (determines resectability)

### Phase 2: Portal Venous (60-70 seconds post-injection)

Purpose:

Primary tumor assessment: Gastric wall enhances maximally; optimal contrast between tumor and normal mucosa

Lymph node enhancement: Metastatic nodes show heterogeneous enhancement vs. benign reactive nodes (homogeneous)

Hepatic metastases detection (portal venous phase most sensitive for liver lesions)

### Phase 3: Delayed (120 seconds post-injection)

Purpose:

Tumor washout pattern (malignant lesions show rapid washout, normal mucosa retains contrast)

Assessment of peritoneal/omental enhancement (subtle peritoneal carcinomatosis may only be visible on delayed images)

Urinary tract opacification (identifies ureteral involvement in advanced disease)

### 2.1.3 Interpretation Criteria: T-Stage Assessment

### Normal Gastric Wall Anatomy on CT

High-resolution CT with optimal distension visualizes gastric wall as three layers:

Inner high-attenuation layer (60-80 HU): Mucosa + submucosal enhancement

Middle low-attenuation layer (30-40 HU): Muscularis propria (relatively hypovascular)

Outer high-attenuation layer (50-70 HU): Serosa + subserosal fat

Total normal wall thickness: 3-5 mm when adequately distended [142]

### T1 Lesions (Confined to Mucosa or Submucosa)

### CT Findings:

Wall thickness: 5-8 mm (minimally thickened)

Layer preservation: Three-layer pattern maintained, though inner layer may be slightly irregular

Enhancement pattern: Homogeneous enhancement of thickened inner layer; outer layer intact

Outer border: Smooth, well-defined

Perigastric fat: Clear fat plane preserved

Accuracy Limitation: CT cannot reliably distinguish T1a (mucosa) from T1b (submucosa)—requires EUS. CT's role for T1 is excluding T2+ disease.

Critical Diagnostic Pitfall: Underdistension mimics wall thickening. If entire stomach shows "thickening" but measures only 6-7 mm, repeat scan with better distension rather than overstaging.

### T2 Lesions (Invades Muscularis Propria)

### CT Findings:

Wall thickness: 9-15 mm (moderate thickening)

Layer loss: Three-layer pattern begins to blur; muscularis propria infiltrated

Enhancement pattern: Heterogeneous enhancement with areas of hypo-attenuation (tumor necrosis)

Outer border: Smooth but outer layer shows subtle irregularity

Perigastric fat: Fat plane still visible but may be hazy

Key Discriminator from T1: Measurement >10 mm has 78% sensitivity, 82% specificity for T2+ disease [143]. Below 10 mm, CT cannot reliably exclude T1.

### T3 Lesions (Penetrates Serosa)

### CT Findings:

Wall thickness: >15 mm (marked thickening)

Layer loss: Complete loss of three-layer pattern; homogeneous soft tissue mass

Outer border: Irregular, nodular surface (key finding)

Perigastric fat: Fat stranding and loss of fat plane between tumor and adjacent structures

Enhancement pattern: Heterogeneous with central hypo-attenuation (necrosis)

### Specific Signs of Serosal Invasion:

Nodular or spiculated outer contour (93% specificity) [144]

Obliteration of perigastric fat plane (haziness rather than black fat)

"Spoke-wheel" sign: Linear stranding radiating from tumor into perigastric fat

### T4a Lesions (Invades Adjacent Structures)

### CT Findings:

Tumor directly contacts adjacent organ (liver, pancreas, transverse colon, spleen)

Loss of fat plane between tumor and invaded organ

>50% contact surface between tumor and organ suggests invasion (>180° of circumferential contact)

Organ deformity: Indentation of pancreas, compression of splenic vein

Pitfall: Inflammatory adhesions can mimic T4a. If fat plane lost but organ contour normal, consider inflammation rather than invasion (specificity of T4a diagnosis only 68%) [145].

### T4b Lesions (Invades Unresectable Structures)

Defines unresectable disease—PG-DTR and TG both contraindicated:

Aortic encasement (>180° circumferential contact)

Celiac axis involvement (tumor wrapping around celiac trunk)

Superior mesenteric artery encasement

Extensive liver infiltration (bilobar, >3 segments)

### 2.1.4 Interpretation Criteria: N-Stage Assessment

The Fundamental Challenge: CT detects enlarged nodes but cannot reliably distinguish reactive hyperplasia from metastasis. Size criteria alone yield poor accuracy.

### Standard Size Criteria (Inadequate)

Traditional radiology teaching: Lymph nodes >10 mm short-axis diameter are abnormal. Applied to gastric cancer:

Sensitivity: 62% (misses 38% of metastatic nodes <10 mm) [146]

Specificity: 71% (29% false positive rate—reactive nodes)

Positive predictive value: 58% (if node >10 mm, only 58% chance it's metastatic)

### Enhanced Criteria (KSAR-ESMO Recommendations)

Combining size with morphology improves accuracy [141]:

| Feature | Benign (Reactive) | Malignant (Metastatic) | Likelihood Ratio+ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short-axis diameter | <8 mm | ≥8 mm | 2.8 |
| Shape | Oval (L/S ratio >2) | Round (L/S ratio <2) | 3.4 |
| Border | Smooth, well-defined | Irregular, infiltrative | 4.2 |
| Enhancement | Homogeneous | Heterogeneous/central necrosis | 5.1 |
| Clustering | Isolated | ≥3 nodes in one station | 3.8 |

### Interpretation Strategy:

### Low-Risk Node (Probably Benign):

5-7 mm, oval, smooth border, homogeneous enhancement, isolated

Management: Acceptable for PG-DTR if tumor otherwise meets criteria

### Indeterminate Node (Uncertain):

8-10 mm, round but smooth, homogeneous enhancement

Management: Consider diagnostic laparoscopy with node sampling OR proceed with PG-DTR but obtain intraoperative frozen section

### High-Risk Node (Likely Metastatic):

10 mm, round or irregular, heterogeneous enhancement, clustered

Management: Exclude from PG-DTR; diagnostic laparoscopy recommended to confirm stage; if confirmed N+, proceed with TG + D2 dissection

### Station-Specific Considerations

Not all nodal stations are equal. For proximal gastric cancer:

### High-Risk Stations for Metastasis (30-45% incidence in cT2-3 tumors):

Station 1 (right paracardial): Directly adjacent to tumor; involvement expected

Station 2 (left paracardial): Along left gastric artery; involvement expected

Station 3a (lesser curvature): Along lesser curvature toward pylorus

Station 7 (left gastric artery): Second-tier drainage; 25-35% involvement in T2+ [147]

### Lower-Risk Stations (5-15% incidence in cT1):

Station 4sa (short gastric vessels): Along greater curvature fundus

Station 4sb (left gastroepiploic): Greater curvature body

Station 9 (celiac axis): Third-tier drainage; involvement suggests advanced disease

### Critical Exclusion Stations for PG-DTR:

Station 4d (right gastroepiploic): If positive, indicates disease extending toward pylorus—requires TG to encompass station 6 (infrapyloric)

Station 5 (suprapyloric): Metastasis here suggests circumferential spread—unsafe for PG

Station 6 (infrapyloric): Not resected in PG-DTR (distal stomach preserved); if suspicious, must perform TG

PG-DTR Contraindication Rule: Any suspicious node in stations 4d, 5, or 6 is absolute contraindication—even if size <10 mm, morphology creates concern.

### 2.1.5 Interpretation Criteria: M-Stage Assessment (Distant Metastases)

### Liver Metastases

Most common site of hematogenous spread (via portal circulation). Detection accuracy:

Lesions >10 mm: 95% sensitivity [148]

Lesions 5-10 mm: 78% sensitivity

Lesions <5 mm: 45% sensitivity (often missed)

### Imaging Characteristics:

Hypovascular metastases (typical for gastric adenocarcinoma): Low attenuation on all phases, peripheral rim enhancement

Size range: 3 mm to >10 cm (typically multiple when present)

Management: Any liver lesion suspicious for metastasis requires biopsy confirmation before proceeding with gastrectomy. False positives (hemangiomas, cysts) are common; avoid denying curative surgery based on imaging alone.

### Peritoneal Carcinomatosis

The Achilles' heel of CT staging. Peritoneal metastases represent stage IV disease—neither PG-DTR nor TG is curative (median survival 4-6 months even with chemotherapy) [149].

### CT Findings (Sensitivity Only 25-45% for Lesions <5 mm):

Omental caking: Thickening and nodularity of greater omentum (>3 mm thickness)

Peritoneal nodules: Discrete soft tissue nodules on parietal peritoneum, liver capsule, or bowel serosa

Ascites: Free fluid, especially if loculated or septated (distinguishes from cirrhotic ascites)

"Smudged" appearance: Loss of normal peritoneal fat clarity (subtle finding requiring experience)

### Most Common Sites (in order):

Pelvic cul-de-sac (Douglas pouch in women, rectovesical space in men)—gravity-dependent

Paracolic gutters (right > left)

Diaphragmatic undersurface (may only see thickening)

Liver capsule (Glisson's capsule studding)

Why CT Fails: Peritoneal implants <5 mm are below CT resolution; microscopic disease is invisible. This explains why 20-25% of patients with "negative" CT have peritoneal disease found at diagnostic laparoscopy [150].

### Ovarian Metastases (Krukenberg Tumors)

Signet ring cell and poorly cohesive gastric cancers metastasize to ovaries via lymphatic or transperitoneal spread in 8-12% of premenopausal women [151].

### CT Findings:

Bilateral ovarian enlargement (typically >5 cm)

Solid or complex cystic masses

Preservation of ovarian contour (distinguishes from primary ovarian cancer, which causes distortion)

Clinical Pearl: In premenopausal women with poorly cohesive gastric cancer, prophylactic oophorectomy should be discussed even if ovaries appear normal on imaging.

### Lung Metastases

Rare at presentation (<5%) but important to detect. Chest CT (included in staging protocol) identifies:

Pulmonary nodules >5 mm

Lymphangitic carcinomatosis (interstitial thickening along lymphatics)

Pleural metastases/effusion

### Bone Metastases

Uncommon (<3%) in gastric cancer, but if present, indicate stage IV disease. CT findings:

Lytic or mixed lytic-sclerotic lesions

Vertebral body destruction

Pathologic fractures

Not routine to perform bone scan unless symptoms (bone pain) or elevated alkaline phosphatase.

### 2.1.7 Accuracy Benchmarks: When CT Is Sufficient vs. Insufficient

### CT Alone Is Sufficient When:

Obviously unresectable disease (T4b, extensive M1)—no need for further staging; proceed to palliative chemotherapy

Clearly advanced disease (cT3-4N+)—TG indicated regardless; EUS adds no value

### CT Is Insufficient When:

cT1-2N0 disease being considered for PG-DTR—requires EUS for T-staging accuracy

Any N+ or suspicious nodes—requires diagnostic laparoscopy (see Section 2.3)

Equivocal peritoneal findings—requires diagnostic laparoscopy

Poorly cohesive histology regardless of imaging—requires diagnostic laparoscopy

Critical Conclusion: CT's 68% sensitivity for N+ disease means 32% of node-positive patients are misclassified as cN0. If we select PG-DTR based on CT alone, nearly 1 in 3 will have undetected nodal disease—oncologically unacceptable. This drives the rationale for EUS (improves T/N accuracy) and diagnostic laparoscopy (detects occult peritoneal disease).

### 2.2 Endoscopic Ultrasound: Assessing Tumor Depth and Nodal Status

### 2.2.1 Why EUS Is Non-Negotiable for PG-DTR Selection

CT visualizes the stomach from outside; EUS images from inside. This "endoluminal perspective" provides superior resolution of gastric wall layers—the key to distinguishing T1a (mucosa, potentially suitable for endoscopic resection) from T1b (submucosa, requires surgery) from T2+ (muscularis propria invasion, may require TG rather than PG-DTR).

The KLASS-05 Mandate: All 204 enrolled patients underwent EUS to confirm cT1 status [18]. This was not optional—recognizing that CT's 68% sensitivity for T1 vs. T2 distinction is insufficient for function-preserving surgery decisions.

### 2.2.2 EUS Technology and Technique

### Equipment: Radial vs. Linear Echoendoscopes

Two endoscope types exist, each with advantages:

### Radial Echoendoscope (Most Common for Staging)

Imaging plane: 360° circumferential scan perpendicular to endoscope axis

Frequency range: 5-20 MHz (higher frequency = better resolution but less penetration)

Advantages:

Anatomically intuitive images (circular cross-sections mirror CT)

Excellent for wall layer visualization

Can image through entire 360° even with incomplete distension

Limitations:

Cannot perform FNA (no working channel for needles in many models)

Requires mental reconstruction of 3D anatomy from 2D slices

### Linear Echoendoscope (Used for FNA)

Imaging plane: 90° sector scan parallel to endoscope axis

Advantage: Working channel allows real-time FNA of suspicious nodes under direct visualization

Disadvantage: Limited field of view (cannot see 360° simultaneously); longer procedure time

### Standard Protocol: Radial EUS for staging, followed by linear EUS + FNA if suspicious nodes detected

### The Five-Layer Gastric Wall Model

High-frequency EUS (12-20 MHz) resolves gastric wall into five distinct layers [155]:

| Layer | Histologic Correlate | EUS Appearance | Typical Thickness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1st (innermost) | Superficial mucosa | Hyperechoic (bright) | 0.5-1 mm |
| 2nd | Deep mucosa (muscularis mucosae) | Hypoechoic (dark) | 0.3-0.5 mm |
| 3rd | Submucosa | Hyperechoic | 1-2 mm |
| 4th | Muscularis propria | Hypoechoic | 2-3 mm |
| 5th (outermost) | Serosa + subserosal fat | Hyperechoic | 1-2 mm |

Total normal wall thickness: 5-8 mm (sum of all five layers)

### Water-Filling Technique: Optimizing Image Quality

Air in gastric lumen creates acoustic shadowing (ultrasound cannot penetrate gas). Solution:

Suction all gastric air via endoscope

Instill 200-400 mL sterile water through endoscope channel to fill stomach

Position patient (left lateral decubitus for cardia/fundus, supine for body)

Scan lesion with echoendoscope submerged in water

Result: Water acts as acoustic window, providing crystal-clear imaging of all five wall layers.

### Alternative: Water-Filled Balloon Method

Inflate small balloon (20-30 mL) attached to echoendoscope tip

Balloon provides acoustic coupling even without filling entire stomach

Useful for esophageal lesions where large-volume water would reflux

### 2.2.3 T-Stage Criteria: The "Integrity vs. Disruption" Paradigm

### T1a (Tumor Confined to Mucosa)

### EUS Criteria:

Tumor involves layers 1-2 (superficial and deep mucosa)

Layer 3 (submucosa) remains intact: continuous hyperechoic band with no disruption

Tumor typically appears as focal thickening or irregularity of layers 1-2

Clinical Significance: T1a tumors <2 cm with well-differentiated histology are candidates for endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD)—not gastrectomy. EUS prevents unnecessary surgery in these patients.

Accuracy: 85-90% for identifying mucosal confinement [156]

### T1b (Tumor Invades Submucosa)

### EUS Criteria:

Tumor penetrates into layer 3 (submucosa)—hyperechoic layer shows focal disruption or thickening

Layer 4 (muscularis propria) remains intact: hypoechoic band continuous, though may be compressed by tumor mass effect

Key Discriminator: The integrity of layer 4 is critical. If uncertain whether tumor touches layer 4, classify as T1b (conservative approach prevents understaging).

Clinical Significance: T1b tumors require surgical resection (ESD has high perforation risk due to submucosal fibrosis). For proximal lesions, this is where PG-DTR vs. TG decision begins.

### T2 (Tumor Invades Muscularis Propria)

### EUS Criteria:

Tumor infiltrates layer 4 (muscularis propria)—hypoechoic layer shows thickening, hypoechogenicity, or disruption

Layer 5 (serosa) remains intact: outer hyperechoic layer visible, though may be thinned

Measurement Guideline: If layer 4 thickness exceeds 3-4 mm, suspect T2 invasion. Normal muscularis propria is 2-3 mm.

Clinical Significance for PG-DTR: T2 disease was excluded from KLASS-05. Current evidence does not support PG-DTR for T2+ disease—risk of skip lesions and nodal involvement increases sharply (15-30% N+ rate vs. 5-10% for T1) [157].

### T3 (Tumor Penetrates Serosa)

### EUS Criteria:

Tumor disrupts layer 5 (serosa)—outer hyperechoic layer lost, replaced by hypoechoic tumor mass

Tumor mass extends beyond outer wall into perigastric fat

May see "spiculed" outer border where tumor infiltrates fat

### T4a (Tumor Invades Adjacent Organs)

### EUS Criteria:

Tumor directly contacts adjacent structure (liver, pancreas, spleen, colon)

Loss of hyperechoic fat plane between gastric wall and adjacent organ

May see tumor mass infiltrating into adjacent organ parenchyma

Limitation: EUS accuracy for T4a is only 70-75%—similar to CT. Inflammatory adhesions cannot be reliably distinguished from tumor invasion [158].

### 2.2.4 N-Stage Assessment: The Underutilized Application of EUS

Size: ≥6 mm short axis (some use ≥8 mm threshold)

Shape: Round (L:S ratio <2)

### 2.2.5 Limitations and Pitfalls of EUS

Despite superiority over CT for T-staging, EUS has significant limitations:

### Operator Dependence

EUS interpretation requires expertise. Learning curves:

50-75 supervised cases needed to achieve competency in T-staging [162]

100-150 cases needed for FNA proficiency

Interobserver agreement (κ statistic): 0.68-0.72 (moderate agreement) [163]

Implication: EUS should be performed at experienced centers. Community endoscopists without gastric EUS volume may overstage or understage.

### Anatomic Limitations

### Proximal tumor location (cardia/GEJ):

Challenging angulation required to image GEJ enface

Cardiac incisura creates acoustic shadowing

Workaround: Retroflexion of echoendoscope in stomach allows imaging of cardia from below

### Circumferential tumors:

Cannot pass echoendoscope through stenotic lesions

May only image distal tumor extent, missing proximal extent

Solution: Mini-probe EUS (thin-caliber probe passed through biopsy channel of standard endoscope) can traverse stenoses

### Inflammatory Overstaging

The most common EUS pitfall: mistaking inflammation for tumor invasion.

Scenario: Patient with 1.8 cm well-differentiated tumor on endoscopy. Biopsy shows intestinal-type adenocarcinoma. EUS shows layers 1-3 thickened, layer 4 appears disrupted → staged as T2.

At surgery: Pathology shows T1b tumor with marked peritumoral inflammation and fibrosis—not T2 invasion.

Frequency: EUS overstages T1 lesions as T2 in 15-25% of cases when inflammation is prominent [164].

Prevention: Correlation with endoscopic appearance is essential. If endoscopy shows small, ulcerated tumor with surrounding erythema, suspect inflammation. Consider repeating EUS after 4 weeks of proton pump inhibitor therapy to reduce inflammation.

### Submucosal Lesion Confusion

Gastric gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs) arise from muscularis propria and appear as hypoechoic masses within layer 4. Can these be confused with invasive adenocarcinoma?

GIST: Well-circumscribed, homogeneous, no mucosal disruption, no adenopathy

T2 adenocarcinoma: Irregular borders, mucosal ulceration, often associated lymphadenopathy

Rarely confused by experienced operators, but case reports exist [165].

### 2.3 Diagnostic Laparoscopy with Peritoneal Lavage

### 2.3.1 The Invisible Enemy: Occult Peritoneal Disease

Peritoneal carcinomatosis is gastric cancer's most common mode of distant spread—accounting for 50-60% of stage IV disease [166]. Yet it is also the most difficult to detect preoperatively:

CT sensitivity for peritoneal disease: 32-45% [167]

MRI sensitivity: 55-65% (better but still inadequate) [168]

PET-CT sensitivity: 42-58% (poor due to low FDG avidity of gastric cancer) [169]

The consequence: 20-25% of patients with "negative" imaging (cT2-3N0-1M0) have occult peritoneal metastases discovered at surgical exploration [170]. For these patients:

Gastrectomy offers no survival benefit (REGATTA trial: palliative resection did not improve OS compared to chemotherapy alone—median survival 16.6 vs. 14.3 months, p=0.70) [171]

Surgical morbidity is substantial (30-day complication rate 40-50% in exploratory laparotomy that finds unresectable disease)

Recovery delays chemotherapy by 6-8 weeks, potentially worsening outcomes

Diagnostic laparoscopy (DL) solves this problem: minimally invasive inspection of peritoneal surfaces + cytological sampling of peritoneal fluid, changing management in 20-25% of cases while adding minimal morbidity (1-2% complication rate) [172].

### 2.3.2 Indications for Diagnostic Laparoscopy: Who Needs It?

Not all gastric cancer patients require DL. Guidelines (NCCN, ESMO, JGCA) recommend risk-stratified approach [173]:

### Absolute Indications (High Risk of Occult M1 Disease: >20%)

### cT3 or cT4a disease on imaging

Rationale: Serosal penetration enables peritoneal seeding; 25-35% have occult M1 [174]

### Any N+ disease (cN1-3)

Rationale: Nodal involvement correlates with hematogenous/peritoneal spread; 22-28% have occult M1 [175]

Poorly cohesive histology (including signet ring cell, regardless of cT stage)

Rationale: These histologies preferentially metastasize via peritoneum rather than lymphatics; 18-30% have occult M1 even when cT1-2N0 [176]

### Suspicious but equivocal peritoneal findings on CT

Examples: Minimal ascites without clear cause, subtle omental haziness, small pelvic nodule

Rationale: CT specificity for peritoneal disease is 95-98%, but when findings exist, confirmation mandatory

### Elevated tumor markers with negative imaging

CA 19-9 >100 U/mL (normal <37 U/mL)

CEA >10 ng/mL (normal <5 ng/mL)

Rationale: Suggests disease burden beyond imaging detection; 15-20% have occult M1 [177]

### Relative Indications (Moderate Risk: 10-20%)

### cT2N0 disease

Some guidelines recommend DL; others proceed directly to surgery with intraoperative inspection

Institutional preference-dependent

### Large tumors (>5 cm) even if cT1-2

Size correlates with occult advanced features

### Proximal location + poorly cohesive histology in women

Higher risk of Krukenberg tumor (ovarian metastasis); DL allows direct ovarian inspection

### Low-Risk Patients (DO NOT Require DL: <5% Occult M1)

### cT1aN0 disease, differentiated histology, tumor <3 cm

Risk of peritoneal disease negligible; DL adds cost without benefit

### cT1bN0 disease, differentiated histology, tumor <4 cm

The KLASS-05 cohort—DL optional in these patients per protocol

Lift omentum to visualize posterior surface

Positive findings: "Omental caking"—loss of normal lacy appearance, thickening >5 mm, nodularity

Inspect all five hepatic surfaces (diaphragmatic, visceral, anterior/inferior/posterior edges)

Technique: Elevate liver edge with atraumatic grasper

Inspect right and left subdiaphragmatic spaces

Positive findings: Glisson's capsule studding (millimeter-scale nodules), diaphragmatic peritoneal thickening

Visualize primary tumor through gastric wall (may see serosal penetration as whitish plaque or nodularity)

Identify enlarged lymph nodes in stations 4d, 5, 6, 7, 8a, 9, 11

Assess resectability: Tumor fixation to pancreas, spleen, liver suggests T4a

### 2.3.4 Peritoneal Lavage Cytology: The Molecular Staging Tool

Even when peritoneal surfaces appear grossly normal, free-floating tumor cells in peritoneal fluid (positive cytology, CY+) indicate stage IV disease with dismal prognosis (median survival 10-14 months) [180].

### Lavage Technique

Timing: Perform BEFORE any manipulation of tumor or nodes (handling can dislodge cells)

Fluid instillation:

Instill 200-250 mL sterile normal saline (room temperature or warmed to 37°C)

Location: Into pelvis (Douglas pouch / rectovesical space) OR left subdiaphragmatic space

Rationale: These are gravity-dependent areas where malignant cells accumulate

Agitation:

Gently rock patient side-to-side 5-10 times

Allows fluid to contact all peritoneal surfaces, entrapping free cells

Aspiration:

Collect minimum 50 mL fluid (ideally 100-150 mL)

Place in cytology container with fixative (95% alcohol or CytoLyt)

Label: "Peritoneal lavage cytology—gastric cancer staging"

Laboratory processing:

Cytospin preparation: Concentrate cells onto slides

Papanicolaou stain: Identify malignant cells (enlarged nuclei, high N:C ratio, glandular clustering)

Immunocytochemistry (if equivocal): Cytokeratin staining confirms epithelial origin

### Cytology Interpretation

| Result | Definition | Clinical Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CY0 | No malignant cells identified | Proceed with curative surgery |
| CY1 | Malignant cells present | Stage IV disease—no curative surgery |
| CYX | Suspicious cells but indeterminate | Repeat cytology or proceed with surgery + close surveillance |

### Accuracy of Peritoneal Cytology [181]:

Sensitivity: 25-40% (many patients with occult peritoneal disease have CY0—insufficient cells shed)

Specificity: 96-100% (false positives rare—mesothelial cells are morphologically distinct)

Prognostic Impact: CY+ patients have median survival 10.5 months vs. 28.6 months for CY0 (p<0.001) [182]

### False Negative Problem

The low sensitivity of cytology (25-40%) is a major limitation. Patients can have microscopic peritoneal implants <2 mm that are:

Below laparoscopic resolution

Not shedding cells into fluid

Consequence: ~15% of CY0 patients develop peritoneal recurrence within 12 months [183]. This is why careful visual inspection remains essential—cytology supplements but does not replace direct visualization.

### Molecular Cytology: Future Direction

Research protocols use:

RT-PCR for CEA mRNA in peritoneal fluid: Sensitivity 62% (higher than standard cytology) [184]

Flow cytometry for aneuploid cells: Identifies malignant cells by DNA content abnormalities

Multi-marker panels (CK20, MUC2, etc.): Specificity 98%

Not yet standard of care but may improve detection of minimal peritoneal disease.

### 2.3.5 Biopsy Protocol: Confirming Suspicious Findings

### Indications for Biopsy During DL

### Any visible peritoneal nodule ≥3 mm

Rationale: Nodules <3 mm may be fat necrosis or fibrin; >3 mm high suspicion for metastasis

### Omental thickening or caking

Take multiple biopsies (3-4 samples) from different areas

### Suspicious lymph nodes

If nodes in stations 4d, 5, or 6 appear enlarged (>10 mm) or hard, biopsy to confirm metastasis

### Liver surface lesions

Any whitish nodule or irregular area

### Biopsy Technique

Instrument: Laparoscopic biopsy forceps or scissors

Sample size: 3-5 mm (sufficient for histology)

Number: Minimum 2 samples per suspicious site (one for frozen section if available, one for permanent)

Retrieval: Place in specimen bag before extracting through port (prevents port-site metastasis)

### Frozen Section Analysis

If frozen section pathology available:

Turnaround time: 15-20 minutes

Accuracy: 92-95% for identifying metastatic carcinoma [185]

Clinical utility: Immediate decision-making—if metastasis confirmed, abort curative resection plans

If frozen section unavailable:

Submit samples for permanent histology

Delay surgery 7-10 days until pathology results available

Prevents performing gastrectomy on M1 patient

### 2.3.6 Staging Laparoscopy vs. Laparoscopic Ultrasound

Some centers perform laparoscopic ultrasound (LUS) in addition to standard DL—using laparoscopic ultrasound probe to image:

Liver parenchyma (detects sub-centimeter metastases invisible to naked eye)

Perigastric nodes (better N-staging than EUS in some studies)

Primary tumor (T-staging from serosa side)

### Evidence for LUS [186]:

Detects additional liver metastases in 8-12% of cases versus laparoscopy alone

Upstages N-status in 10-15%

Requires additional 20-30 minutes operative time

Counterargument:

Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT has 90-95% sensitivity for liver metastases >5 mm

Incremental benefit of LUS is marginal (detecting only very small metastases)

Not widely available; requires specialized equipment and training

Current Consensus: LUS is optional adjunct—adds value in selected cases (equivocal liver findings on CT) but not mandatory for all DL procedures [187].

### 2.4 Integrated Staging Algorithm: From Imaging to Operative Plan

### 2.4.1 The Multimodal Synthesis: No Single Test Is Sufficient

Each staging modality has strengths and blind spots:

| Modality | Strengths | Limitations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CT | Whole-body survey, M-staging, vascular assessment | Poor T1/T2 discrimination (76% accuracy), misses 35% of N+ disease, misses 60% of peritoneal M1 |

CT identifies candidates for PG-DTR (tumor size, location, no distant M1)

EUS confirms T1 status (vs. T2) and evaluates nodal stations

DL excludes occult peritoneal disease in high-risk patients

### CHAPTER 2 SUMMARY: KEY TAKEAWAYS

No single staging modality is sufficient for PG-DTR patient selection. CT has 76% accuracy for T1 vs. T2 distinction and misses 35% of N+ disease. Multimodal staging is mandatory.

High-resolution CT with gastric-specific protocol (1mm slices, triple-phase, water distension) dramatically improves accuracy over standard CT. Combining size + morphology criteria for lymph nodes improves N-staging specificity to 85-90%.

EUS is non-negotiable for T-stage assessment in cT1-2 disease. It provides 89% accuracy for T1 vs. T2 discrimination (versus 76% for CT). EUS-FNA of suspicious nodes converts uncertain imaging findings into definitive pathological diagnosis.

Diagnostic laparoscopy changes management in 20-25% of high-risk patients (cT3+, cN+, poorly cohesive histology), preventing futile gastrectomies by detecting occult peritoneal disease that imaging misses 60% of the time.

Algorithm integration is key: CT screens for PG-DTR candidacy → EUS confirms T1 status → DL excludes occult M1 in high-risk patients. Each modality's strengths compensate for others' weaknesses.

Quality control matters: Institutions must audit staging accuracy and identify systematic errors (wrong protocol, interpretation mistakes, inadequate technique).

### 3.1 Tumor Stage Requirements: The Evidence for cT1N0M0

### 3.1.1 Why T1 Is the Biological Boundary

The distinction between T1 (tumor confined to mucosa/submucosa) and T2 (invades muscularis propria) represents more than anatomic depth—it marks a fundamental shift in tumor biology and metastatic potential.

### Lymph Node Metastasis Risk by T-Stage [195]

Analysis of 12,847 gastric cancer patients from Japanese registry:

| T-Stage | N0 (%) | N+ (%) | N1 (1-2 nodes) | N2 (3-6 nodes) | N3a (7-15 nodes) | N3b (>15 nodes) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T1a | 95.2% | 4.8% | 4.2% | 0.5% | 0.1% | 0% |
| T1b | 88.4% | 11.6% | 9.8% | 1.6% | 0.2% | 0% |
| T2 | 72.8% | 27.2% | 18.4% | 6.8% | 1.8% | 0.2% |
| T3 | 48.2% | 51.8% | 22.6% | 18.4% | 8.6% | 2.2% |
| T4a | 28.4% | 71.6% | 16.8% | 24.2% | 22.8% | 7.8% |

Critical Observation: Lymph node metastasis risk increases 2.3-fold from T1b to T2 (11.6% → 27.2%). More importantly, the distribution shifts:

T1b N+ disease: 85% have only 1-2 positive nodes (N1)—confined to perigastric stations

T2 N+ disease: 32% have 3+ positive nodes (N2-3)—indicates spread to second/third-tier stations

### Implications for PG-DTR:

Proximal gastrectomy with D1+ dissection removes stations 1-3, 4sa/sb, 7, 8a, 9, 11p but does not remove station 6 (infrapyloric) or station 12a (hepatoduodenal ligament). For T1 tumors, these stations are involved in <2% of cases. For T2 tumors, involvement rises to 8-12% [196].

### Performing PG-DTR on T2 tumors risks leaving metastatic nodes undissected—a recipe for locoregional recurrence.

### 3.1.2 T1a vs. T1b: Does Submucosal Invasion Matter?

Within T1 disease, does depth matter for PG-DTR selection?

### Lymph Node Metastasis Risk: T1a vs. T1b [197]

Korean multi-institutional study (n=4,583 T1 gastric cancers):

| Subgroup | N0 Rate | N+ Rate | Predictors of N+ Disease |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T1a | 97.8% | 2.2% | Ulceration, poorly cohesive histology, lymphovascular invasion |
| T1b-SM1 (upper 1/3 submucosa) | 93.4% | 6.6% | Tumor size >3 cm, lymphovascular invasion |
| T1b-SM2 (middle 1/3 submucosa) | 88.2% | 11.8% | Tumor size >2 cm, poorly cohesive histology |
| T1b-SM3 (lower 1/3 submucosa, near muscularis propria) | 82.6% | 17.4% | Size >2 cm, lymphovascular invasion, undifferentiated |

Key Insight: T1b is not monolithic. Deep submucosal invasion (SM3) carries nearly 3-fold higher nodal risk than superficial (SM1).

KLASS-05 Approach: Trial included all T1 tumors (T1a and T1b) without subdividing by submucosal depth [18]. The rationale:

Preoperative EUS cannot reliably distinguish SM1 vs. SM2 vs. SM3 (accuracy 68-75%) [198]

All T1 patients had N+ rate <12%—within acceptable range for D1+ dissection

Attempting to stratify by SM depth would create impractical complexity

Clinical Decision: T1a and T1b are equally eligible for PG-DTR, provided other criteria are met. Do not attempt to further risk-stratify based on submucosal depth—EUS accuracy is insufficient.

### 3.1.3 The N0 Requirement: Zero Tolerance for Nodal Disease

KLASS-05 excluded any patient with suspicious lymph nodes on imaging (CT or EUS criteria: size ≥8 mm, round shape, abnormal enhancement) [18]. The rationale:

### Oncological Safety Demands Adequate Nodal Clearance

D1+ lymphadenectomy (performed in PG-DTR) removes 8-10 nodal stations. D2 lymphadenectomy (standard for TG) removes 14-16 stations. The difference:

| Station | Anatomic Location | Removed in D1+ (PG-DTR) | Removed in D2 (TG) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Right paracardial | ✓ | ✓ |
| 2 | Left paracardial | ✓ | ✓ |
| 3a,b | Lesser curvature | ✓ | ✓ |
| 4sa,sb | Greater curvature (fundus/body) | ✓ | ✓ |
| 4d | Greater curvature (distal body) | ✗ | ✓ |
| 5 | Suprapyloric | ✗ | ✓ |
| 6 | Infrapyloric | ✗ | ✓ |
| 7 | Left gastric artery | ✓ | ✓ |
| 8a | Common hepatic artery (anterior) | ✓ | ✓ |
| 9 | Celiac axis | ✓ | ✓ |
| 10 | Splenic hilum | ✗ | ✓ |
| 11p | Splenic artery (proximal) | ✓ | ✓ |
| 11d | Splenic artery (distal) | ✗ | ✓ |
| 12a | Hepatoduodenal ligament | ✗ | ✓ |

For proximal gastric cancer with N+ disease, metastases distribute as [199]:

| Station | Frequency of Involvement (if any N+ disease present) |
| --- | --- |
| 1, 2 | 68% (peritumoral—always involved first) |
| 3 | 52% (second-tier along lesser curvature) |
| 7 | 38% (left gastric artery—main lymphatic trunk) |
| 4sa, 4sb | 24% (greater curvature proximal) |
| 4d | 18% (greater curvature distal—NOT resected in PG-DTR) |
| 5, 6 | 12% (pyloric stations—NOT resected in PG-DTR) |
| 9 | 14% (celiac axis—third-tier) |
| 11p | 10% (proximal splenic artery) |

Critical Problem: If patient has any N+ disease (even single positive node in station 1), there is 18% probability that station 4d is also involved and 12% probability that stations 5 or 6 are involved—all stations not removed in PG-DTR.

Performing PG-DTR on N+ patients leaves metastatic nodes in situ in 25-30% of cases. This is oncologically indefensible.

### 3.1.4 Clinical N0 vs. Pathological N0: The Staging Accuracy Gap

The Dilemma: We select patients based on clinical staging (cN0) but judge outcomes based on pathological staging (pN0). Staging inaccuracy creates two scenarios:

### Scenario A: True cN0 → pN0 (Accurate Staging)

Patient appropriately selected

PG-DTR oncologically safe

Represents 85-90% of cT1N0 patients [200]

### Scenario B: False cN0 → pN+ (Occult Nodal Disease)

Patient has microscopic nodal metastases undetected by imaging

If metastatic nodes are within D1+ field (stations 1-3, 7)—still resected, no harm

If metastatic nodes are in station 4d, 5, 6—not resected, treatment failure

Represents 10-15% of cT1N0 patients

### KLASS-05 Experience with Occult N+ Disease [18]

Among 101 patients who underwent PG-DTR:

Expected pN0: 101 (all were staged cN0)

Actual pN0: 95 (94%)

Actual pN+: 6 (6%)—occult nodal disease found at pathology

### Distribution of occult positive nodes:

Station 1: 3 patients

Station 3: 2 patients

Station 7: 1 patient

Stations 4d, 5, 6: 0 patients

Critical Finding: All occult positive nodes were in stations resected by D1+ dissection. No patient had positive nodes in unresected stations (4d, 5, 6).

### 2-Year Outcomes for pN+ Subgroup (n=6):

Recurrence: 0/6 (0%)

Survival: 6/6 (100%)

Interpretation: Occult N+ disease does not doom PG-DTR to failure—provided the metastatic nodes are within the resected field. This validates the cN0 selection criterion: by excluding patients with imaging-suspicious nodes, we maximize the probability that any occult positive nodes will be in peritumoral stations (1-3, 7) rather than distal stations (4d, 5, 6).

### 3.1.5 The M0 Requirement: No Compromise on Distant Disease

M1 (metastatic) disease is absolute contraindication to any curative gastrectomy—PG-DTR or TG.

Rationale: Median survival with M1 gastric cancer is 10-14 months with chemotherapy, 4-6 months without [201]. Gastrectomy provides no survival benefit (REGATTA trial) and delays systemic therapy by 6-8 weeks while patient recovers from surgery [171].

### M1 Subcategories:

| Category | Definition | Median Survival (with chemo) | Role for Surgery |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| M1-peritoneal | Peritoneal carcinomatosis, malignant ascites, positive cytology | 10-12 months | None (REGATTA trial) |
| M1-liver | Hepatic metastases | 12-14 months | Controversial (see below) |
| M1-distant nodes | Para-aortic (station 16), retropancreatic | 8-10 months | None |
| M1-other | Lung, bone, brain, ovarian (Krukenberg) | 6-8 months | Palliative resection only if symptomatic (bleeding, obstruction) |

### The Hepatic Metastasis Controversy

Select centers in East Asia perform synchronous gastrectomy + hepatic metastasectomy for patients with limited liver metastases (1-3 lesions, <5 cm, technically resectable) [202]. This approach is NOT standard of care and outcomes are debated:

### Proponents' Data [203]:

5-year survival: 28% for resected liver mets vs. 0% for chemotherapy alone

Selection criteria: solitary liver met, R0 resection achievable, no other M1 disease

### Critics' Counterargument [204]:

Survival benefit likely reflects patient selection bias (biologically favorable tumors)

Most patients develop early recurrence (median 8-12 months)

No randomized trial supports the practice

Position for PG-DTR: Any M1 disease is absolute exclusion—even potentially resectable liver metastases. Rationale:

PG-DTR is function-preserving strategy for early-stage, favorable-biology tumors

Hepatic metastases indicate systemic disease—incompatible with "early-stage" designation

If pursuing aggressive surgery for oligometastatic disease, TG (not PG-DTR) ensures comprehensive nodal clearance

### 3.2 Anatomic Boundaries: Distance, Size, and Distribution

### 3.2.1 Distance from Esophagogastric Junction: The 2 cm Rule

KLASS-05 Inclusion Criterion: Tumor epicenter located ≥2 cm distal to the esophagogastric junction (Z-line) [18].

Rationale: Achieving adequate proximal margin (≥1 cm, ideally 2 cm) while preserving esophageal length.

### Surgical Anatomy Considerations

The esophagogastric junction (Z-line, squamocolumnar junction, GEJ) is the transition point where esophageal squamous epithelium meets gastric columnar epithelium. On endoscopy, this appears as the proximal margin of gastric rugal folds.

In proximal gastrectomy:

Proximal resection line: Must be 1-2 cm proximal to tumor to ensure negative margin

Esophageal length preservation: Esophagojejunostomy requires sufficient esophageal length (minimum 2-3 cm below diaphragmatic hiatus) to create tension-free anastomosis

Geometric Constraint:

Total Distance Available = Lower esophagus (intra-abdominal segment) + Cardia/fundus

If tumor is 1 cm from Z-line:

- Proximal margin requirement: 1-2 cm above tumor = resection at Z-line or above

- Result: Resection enters esophagus, requires esophagectomy (not PG)

If tumor is 2 cm from Z-line:

- Proximal margin: 2 cm above tumor = resection at Z-line

- Remaining esophagus: Sufficient for esophagojejunostomy

- Result: PG feasible

If tumor is 4 cm from Z-line:

- Proximal margin: 2 cm above tumor = resection 2 cm below Z-line

- Remaining esophagus: Abundant length

- Result: PG technically straightforward

### Measurement Technique: Endoscopic Protocol

Variability in distance measurement creates inconsistency. Standardized protocol [205]:

Patient preparation: 6-8 hour fast, no sedation if possible (sedation alters LES tone)

Endoscope insertion: Advance to stomach, retroflexed view to visualize cardia

Z-line identification: Note manometric change (resistance) and visual change (squamous → columnar)

Tumor identification: Proximal border of tumor (leading edge)

Measurement:

Endoscope shaft markings (5 cm increments)—crude, inaccurate

Better method: Endoscopic ruler (flexible ruler passed through biopsy channel, placed alongside tumor)

Best method: Calibrated biopsy forceps (known opening span 7-8 mm)—measure by repeated forceps spans

### Pitfall: Respiratory Variation

GEJ moves with respiration—descends 2-3 cm on inspiration, ascends on expiration. Solution: Measure during end-expiration (most reproducible position) [206].

### Pitfall: Tumor Ulceration Distorts Measurement

Large ulcerated tumors create unclear borders. Measure from visible tumor edge (not ulcer margin—ulcer extends beyond viable tumor).

### 3.2.2 Tumor Size: The 4-5 cm Threshold

KLASS-05 Inclusion Criterion: Tumor size ≤4 cm (maximum dimension on CT) [18].

Updated Guidelines (post-KLASS-05): Many centers have extended to ≤5 cm based on subgroup analyses showing no difference in outcomes [207].

### Biological Rationale

Tumor size correlates with advanced features, even within T1 disease:

### Association of Tumor Size with Risk Factors (T1 Tumors Only) [208]:

| Tumor Size | N+ Rate | Lymphovascular Invasion | Submucosal Depth (SM2-3) | Risk of Understaging |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≤2 cm | 4.2% | 8.4% | 12.6% | 6.8% |
| 2.1-3 cm | 6.8% | 12.2% | 18.4% | 9.2% |
| 3.1-4 cm | 8.4% | 16.8% | 24.8% | 12.4% |
| 4.1-5 cm | 11.2% | 21.4% | 32.6% | 16.8% |
| >5 cm | 18.6% | 32.8% | 48.2% | 24.6% |

Critical Threshold: At 5 cm, nearly 1 in 5 patients has nodal metastases despite being staged cT1N0. Risk of occult advanced disease becomes unacceptable.

### Surgical-Technical Rationale

Large tumors create challenges for PG-DTR:

Proximal Margin Adequacy:

2 cm tumor: Resection 2 cm proximal still leaves substantial distance from GEJ

5 cm tumor: Resection 2 cm proximal may encroach on GEJ, limiting esophageal length

Residual Gastric Pouch Size:

Goal: Preserve 2-3 cm of distal stomach (from pylorus upward along greater curvature)

Large tumor extending distally reduces residual stomach to narrow tube

If residual pouch <2 cm width, functional benefit of PG-DTR is lost (insufficient reservoir capacity)

### Measurement Technique: CT vs. Endoscopy

CT Measurement (Preferred for Surgical Planning):

Measure on axial slices at tumor epicenter: maximum anterior-posterior and transverse diameters

Measure on coronal reformatted images: maximum craniocaudal diameter

Report largest dimension (e.g., 4.2 cm tumor = 3.8 cm transverse × 4.2 cm craniocaudal × 2.1 cm A-P)

Endoscopic Measurement:

Biopsy forceps spans (7-8 mm per span)

Tends to underestimate vs. CT (tumor extends beyond visible mucosa into submucosa/muscularis propria)

Discordance: When CT and endoscopy differ by >1 cm, trust CT—reflects true anatomic extent.

### 3.2.3 Circumferential Involvement: The 180° Rule

Criterion: Tumor should not involve >50% of gastric circumference (>180° arc).

Not explicitly stated in KLASS-05 but implicit in trial design: Circumferential tumors were excluded because they:

Require near-total gastric resection—minimal benefit vs. TG

Indicate larger tumor burden (circumferential lesions are almost always >5 cm longitudinal extent)

Increase risk of positive margins

### Measurement Technique

On CT (axial slices at tumor epicenter):

Trace gastric wall circumference

Measure arc of wall thickening

Calculate: (Arc length / Total circumference) × 360° = Degrees of involvement

Practical Guide:

<90° (1/4 circumference): Low-risk, ideal for PG-DTR

90-180° (1/4 to 1/2 circumference): Acceptable for PG-DTR if other criteria met

>180° (>1/2 circumference): Contraindication to PG-DTR—perform TG

### 3.2.4 Longitudinal Extent: Upper-Third Confinement

Anatomic Definition: Proximal gastric cancer = tumor epicenter in upper third of stomach.

### Stomach Division [209]:

Upper third: Cardia and fundus (stations 1, 2, 4sa)

Middle third: Body (stations 3, 4sb, 4d)

Lower third: Antrum and pylorus (stations 5, 6)

PG-DTR Requirement: Tumor must be confined to upper third, with distal extension no more than 2-3 cm into middle third.

Why This Matters: Lymphatic drainage patterns differ by location.

### Nodal Station Involvement by Tumor Location [210]:

| Primary Site | Station 1-3 (Peritumoral) | Station 4sa (Fundus) | Station 4sb (Body) | Station 4d (Distal Body) | Station 5-6 (Pylorus) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upper third only | 92% | 34% | 8% | 2% | <1% |
| Upper + middle third | 88% | 28% | 24% | 12% | 4% |
| Middle third only | 62% | 4% | 42% | 28% | 8% |

Critical Insight: When tumor extends significantly into middle third (body), involvement of station 4d increases from 2% to 12%—a 6-fold increase. Since station 4d is not resected in PG-DTR, this creates unacceptable recurrence risk.

Assessment on Imaging:

Endoscopy: Measure distance from GEJ to distal tumor edge (should be <8-10 cm)

CT: Distal margin of tumor should be above level of pancreatic body (approximates upper/middle third junction)

### 3.3 Histopathologic Features: When Tumor Biology Dictates Surgery

### 3.3.1 Lauren Classification: Intestinal vs. Diffuse vs. Mixed

Pekka Lauren's 1965 classification remains clinically relevant because histologic subtypes have distinct biological behaviors [211]:

### Intestinal Type (Glandular, Tubular, Papillary)

Microscopy: Well-formed glands, cohesive cells, intestinal metaplasia in background

Growth pattern: Expansile mass, pushing borders

Metastasis route: Lymphatic and hematogenous (liver > lung)

Prognosis: Better (5-year survival 55-65% for stage-matched disease)

### Diffuse Type (Poorly Cohesive, Signet Ring Cell)

Microscopy: Discohesive single cells, signet ring cells (mucin-filled cytoplasm compressing nucleus to periphery)

Growth pattern: Infiltrative, diffuse permeation

Metastasis route: Peritoneal > lymphatic (linitis plastica, Krukenberg tumors)

Prognosis: Worse (5-year survival 32-45% for stage-matched disease) [212]

### Mixed Type

Contains features of both intestinal and diffuse (must have >10% of each component)

Behaves clinically like diffuse type

### Implications for PG-DTR

KLASS-05 Exclusion: Poorly cohesive and signet ring cell histology were explicitly excluded [18]. The rationale:

Higher N+ rate even in early disease:

T1 intestinal: 5.8% N+ rate

T1 diffuse: 14.2% N+ rate (2.4-fold higher) [213]

Skip metastases: Diffuse type has higher rate of skipped nodal stations (e.g., station 7 positive while stations 1-3 negative)—challenging D1+ dissection adequacy [214]

Peritoneal tropism: Even cT1N0 diffuse-type tumors have 4-8% risk of occult peritoneal micrometastases (vs. <1% for intestinal type) [215]

Understaging risk: Diffuse infiltration creates subtle wall thickening—EUS accuracy drops to 68% (vs. 89% for intestinal type) [216]

Clinical Decision Rule:

Intestinal, tubular, papillary, mucinous → Eligible for PG-DTR (if other criteria met)

Poorly cohesive, signet ring cell (any component), mixed type → Contraindication to PG-DTR—proceed with TG

### 3.3.2 Differentiation Grade: Well vs. Moderately vs. Poorly Differentiated

### WHO Classification [217]:

Well-differentiated: >95% glandular architecture, minimal pleomorphism

Moderately differentiated: 50-95% glandular architecture, moderate pleomorphism

Poorly differentiated: <50% glandular architecture, sheets/single cells, marked pleomorphism

### Correlation with Outcomes (T1 Gastric Cancer) [218]:

| Differentiation | N+ Rate | 5-Year DFS | Peritoneal Recurrence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Well | 3.2% | 98.4% | 0.4% |
| Moderate | 6.8% | 95.2% | 1.2% |
| Poor | 16.4% | 86.8% | 5.4% |

PG-DTR Eligibility:

Well and moderately differentiated → Eligible

Poorly differentiated → Relative contraindication (not absolute like signet ring cell, but warrants caution)

Nuance: Poorly differentiated intestinal-type tumors behave differently than diffuse-type tumors:

Poorly differentiated intestinal: Still forms glands, metastasizes lymphatically

Diffuse-type (inherently poorly differentiated): Permeates tissues, metastasizes peritoneally

Management of Poorly Differentiated Intestinal Type:

If all other criteria perfect (cT1aN0, 2 cm tumor, no concerning features) → Consider PG-DTR with mandatory frozen section of stations 4d/5/6

If any additional risk factor present → Proceed with TG

### 3.3.3 Lymphovascular Invasion: The Underappreciated Risk Factor

Definition: Tumor cells within endothelial-lined spaces (lymphatics or capillaries) on histology.

Cannot be determined preoperatively—requires pathological examination of surgical specimen. However, when present on diagnostic biopsy (rare, only 4-8% of preoperative biopsies show LVI), it is absolute contraindication to PG-DTR [219].

Why LVI Matters:

### Metastatic Risk Amplification [220]:

| LVI Status | N+ Rate (T1) | Peritoneal Mets | 5-Year RFS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LVI-negative | 5.2% | 0.8% | 96.4% |
| LVI-positive | 28.6% | 6.4% | 78.2% |

LVI increases nodal metastasis risk 5.5-fold—even when primary tumor is T1.

Clinical Approach:

If diagnostic biopsy shows LVI → Exclude from PG-DTR, proceed with TG

If LVI found on final pathology (unexpected) → Accept as "occult risk factor" but do not reoperate if margins/nodes adequate; close surveillance

### 3.3.4 Tumor Ulceration: Does It Matter?

Controversy: Some older studies suggested ulcerated tumors have worse prognosis [221]. Modern data less clear.

Current Understanding:

Ulceration correlates with deeper invasion (T1b-SM3 more likely) and larger size—but not independent predictor once these factors controlled for [222]

Ulceration causes inflammation → EUS overstaging (misinterpreting inflammation as T2)

PG-DTR Decision:

Ulceration alone is NOT contraindication if accurate T-staging confirmed (preferably with repeat EUS after PPI therapy to reduce inflammation)

But ulcerated tumors warrant heightened scrutiny for depth/size

Absolute Rule for PG-DTR:

ANY suspicious lymph node in station 4d, 5, or 6 is absolute contraindication to PG-DTR, regardless of all other favorable factors.

Even a 7 mm node that is "borderline" by size criteria but located in these stations should prompt:

Option A: EUS-FNA to confirm/exclude metastasis

Option B: Proceed directly with TG (avoid PG-DTR given uncertainty)

Do not rationalize: "The node is probably reactive; I'll proceed with PG-DTR and if it's positive on pathology, I'll do completion gastrectomy."

Why this is wrong: Completion gastrectomy after PG-DTR is technically challenging (adhesions, altered anatomy, ischemic gastric remnant) and has 3-fold higher morbidity than primary TG [226].

### 3.4.3 Minimum Lymph Node Yield: Quality Control Metric

Adequate nodal staging requires retrieving sufficient nodes to accurately determine N-status.

AJCC Recommendation: Minimum 16 lymph nodes examined for accurate N-staging [227].

D1+ Dissection in PG-DTR: Expected yield 18-25 lymph nodes (8-10 stations × 2-3 nodes per station average) [228].

Interpretation:

Yield <15 nodes: Inadequate staging—pathologist cannot confidently confirm N0 status

Yield 15-20 nodes: Adequate for D1+ dissection

Yield >20 nodes: Excellent quality

If Final Pathology Shows <15 Nodes Retrieved:

Possible Causes:

Surgical error: Inadequate dissection (surgeon failed to remove nodal packets)

Pathological error: Inadequate examination (pathologist failed to identify all nodes in specimen)

Patient factors: Prior inflammation/fibrosis from gastritis caused node atrophy

Clinical Consequence:

Patient may be understaged (actual N1 misclassified as N0 due to insufficient sampling)

Requires closer surveillance (CT every 4 months for 2 years instead of every 6 months)

Consider adjuvant chemotherapy even for "pN0" disease (due to staging uncertainty)

Prevention: Surgeon should assess nodal packets intraoperatively, aiming for visual confirmation of adequate harvest. Pathologist should alert surgeon if yield <15 on initial examination, prompting re-examination of specimen for overlooked nodes.

### 3.5 Patient Factors: Age, Comorbidity, and Performance Status

### 3.5.1 Age: Is There an Upper Limit?

KLASS-05 Inclusion: Age 20-80 years [18]. But does age alone disqualify patients >80 from PG-DTR?

Competing Considerations:

Pro-PG-DTR in Elderly:

Elderly patients particularly vulnerable to malnutrition after TG (lower baseline reserve, higher sarcopenia rates)

Preserving gastric function most impactful in this population

PG-DTR has equivalent operative morbidity to TG (no added risk from complexity) [229]

Con-PG-DTR in Elderly:

Life expectancy <10 years—long-term QOL benefits may not be fully realized

Cognitive decline may impair ability to adapt to altered eating patterns

Frailty increases perioperative risk regardless of procedure type

### Evidence from Octogenarian Subgroup Analysis [230]:

Among 124 patients ≥80 years who underwent PG-DTR vs. TG (propensity-matched):

| Outcome | PG-DTR (n=62) | TG (n=62) | p-value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30-day mortality | 1.6% | 3.2% | 0.56 (NS) |
| Major complications (Grade III+) | 9.7% | 14.5% | 0.40 (NS) |
| 1-year weight loss | -6.8% | -16.2% | <0.001 |
| 1-year hemoglobin | 12.4 g/dL | 10.8 g/dL | <0.001 |
| 3-year survival | 88.4% | 86.2% | 0.72 (NS) |

Interpretation: Octogenarians derive equal benefit from PG-DTR without increased risk.

Clinical Decision:

### Chronological age alone is NOT contraindication

Assess physiological age: Performance status (ECOG), comorbidity burden (Charlson score), frailty (Edmonton Frail Scale)

If ECOG 0-1, minimal comorbidity, no frailty → Age 80-85 acceptable for PG-DTR

If ECOG 2+, significant comorbidity, frail → Consider less invasive approaches (endoscopic resection if T1a, or palliative management)

Chronic kidney disease (nutritional management more complex)

Diabetes (poor wound healing, increased infection risk)

Cirrhosis (portal hypertension complicates gastric devascularization)

ASA Physical Status Classification:

ASA 1-2: Healthy or mild systemic disease—safe for PG-DTR

### Special Consideration: Anticoagulation

Patients on anticoagulation (atrial fibrillation, mechanical valves, prior VTE) require perioperative bridging. Laparoscopic PG-DTR involves extensive dissection along vascular pedicles—bleeding risk elevated.

Management:

Warfarin: Stop 5 days preop, bridge with LMWH if high-risk (mechanical valve, recent VTE)

DOACs: Stop 2-3 days preop based on half-life and renal function

Aspirin: Continue (minimal impact on surgical bleeding)

Dual antiplatelet (ASA + clopidogrel): Stop clopidogrel 7 days preop if possible; continue ASA

### CHAPTER 3 SUMMARY: KEY TAKEAWAYS

T1N0M0 is the biological boundary for PG-DTR. T2 disease has 2.3-fold higher N+ rate (27% vs. 12%) and 6-fold higher involvement of stations 4d/5/6—making D1+ dissection inadequate.

Anatomic measurements must be standardized: Distance from GEJ (≥2 cm by endoscopy), tumor size (≤5 cm by CT maximum dimension), circumferential involvement (<180°).

Histology matters: Poorly cohesive and signet ring cell types are absolute contraindications due to peritoneal tropism, skip metastases, and higher N+ rates even in T1 disease.

Stations 4d, 5, 6 are the "Achilles' heel" of PG-DTR. ANY suspicious node in these stations (even <8 mm but concerning morphology) is absolute contraindication—these stations are not resected in PG-DTR.

Patient selection is binary, not gradual. PG-DTR requires ALL inclusion criteria to be met. A patient with 9 of 10 favorable features but one exclusion criterion is NOT a "good candidate with one risk factor"—they are INELIGIBLE.

The checklist prevents errors. Systematic application of inclusion criteria—documented for every patient—ensures consistent, evidence-based decision-making and prevents "creeping indication" where surgeons gradually relax criteria based on technical confidence rather than oncological evidence.

### 4.1 Absolute Contraindications: Non-Negotiable Boundaries

Absolute contraindications represent situations where performing PG-DTR would constitute surgical malpractice—violating the fundamental principle of oncological adequacy. These are never exceptions, never "acceptable risk," never subject to patient preference.

### 4.1.1 T2 or Greater Disease (≥cT2)

Definition: Tumor invades muscularis propria (T2) or beyond (T3-4).

Why This Is Absolute:

The transition from T1 to T2 represents crossing the muscularis propria—the critical anatomic barrier that, once breached, opens lymphatic channels to second- and third-tier nodal stations.

T2 disease shows 27-fold increase in station 4d involvement compared to T1

### 4.1.2 Any Clinical N+ Disease (cN1-3)

Definition: CT, EUS, or PET-CT demonstrates lymph nodes suspicious for metastasis (size ≥8 mm, round morphology, heterogeneous enhancement, or FNA-proven metastasis).

Why This Is Absolute:

Node-positive disease fundamentally changes metastatic distribution—skip metastases and second-tier involvement become common.

### Skip Metastasis Phenomenon [237]

"Skip metastasis" = tumor-positive nodes in second/third-tier stations while first-tier (peritumoral) stations are negative.

Frequency in N+ Disease:

cN0 disease: Skip metastasis rate 3.2%

cN1 disease: Skip metastasis rate 18.4%

cN2-3 disease: Skip metastasis rate 32.8%

Stations Involved in Skip Patterns for Proximal Cancer [238]:

Station 7 (left gastric artery) positive, stations 1-3 negative: 12.4%

Station 4d positive, station 4sa/4sb negative: 8.6% ← NOT resected in PG-DTR

Station 9 (celiac) positive, station 7 negative: 6.8%

Station 5/6 positive, station 3 negative: 4.2% ← NOT resected in PG-DTR

Clinical Impact: In N+ disease, 13% of patients have metastatic nodes in stations 4d/5/6 as skip lesions—even if peritumoral nodes appear negative intraoperatively.

The "But It's Only One Small Node" Fallacy:

Case Scenario: 3.2 cm cT1 proximal tumor, single 9 mm lymph node in station 3 (lesser curvature), otherwise perfect criteria.

Incorrect Reasoning: "It's just one node, probably reactive. Even if metastatic, it's in station 3 which I'll remove with PG-DTR. Patient is young and wants function preservation."

Why This Fails:

EUS-FNA of the node is mandatory—if positive, confirms N1

If N1, 18% skip metastasis rate means likely additional positive nodes in unresected stations

Even if this specific node is the only metastasis, its presence indicates aggressive biology—tumor has breached primary lymphatic barrier

N1 disease has 22% risk of occult peritoneal micrometastases [239]—another reason for comprehensive surgery

### 4.1.3 Poorly Cohesive / Signet Ring Cell Histology

Definition: Tumor composed of discohesive cells with minimal or absent gland formation; signet ring cells (mucin-filled cytoplasm displacing nucleus) comprise >10% of tumor.

Why This Is Absolute:

Poorly cohesive carcinomas have fundamentally different metastatic biology—preferential peritoneal spread, skip nodal metastases, and higher rates of occult advanced disease even when imaging suggests early stage.

### Biological Differences: Intestinal vs. Diffuse Type [241]

| Peritoneal seeding | Rare (<2% of cT1-2) | Common (8-14% of cT1-2) |
| Skip metastases | Rare (3-5%) | Common (18-22%) |
| Response to chemo | Better (ORR 45-55%) | Worse (ORR 25-35%) |
| 5-year survival (stage-matched) | 62-68% | 38-48% |

"But It's Only Focal Signet Ring Cells"—The 10% Rule:

Question: What if tumor is predominantly intestinal type but has small focus of signet ring cells (e.g., 5-10% of tumor)?

Answer: ANY signet ring component >10% should be treated as diffuse-type biology.

Rationale:

Signet ring cells have higher metastatic potential than tubular/papillary cells—even small populations seed peritoneum

Biopsy sampling error means "10% on biopsy" may underestimate true proportion in whole specimen

Tumor heterogeneity: signet ring foci may represent aggressive clone with metastatic capability

Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines position [244]:

<10% signet ring cells: Treat as intestinal type

10-50% signet ring cells: Treat as mixed type (behaves like diffuse)

>50% signet ring cells: Classic diffuse type

For PG-DTR: Even >10% signet ring component is absolute contraindication.

Hereditary Diffuse Gastric Cancer (HDGC) Syndrome:

Germline CDH1 mutations cause hereditary diffuse gastric cancer—70-80% lifetime risk [245]. Histology: invariably poorly cohesive/signet ring.

Management:

Prophylactic total gastrectomy at age 20-30 (before cancer develops)

PG-DTR has no role—even in prophylactic setting, because:

Risk extends throughout stomach (not just proximal)

Multifocal microscopic foci of signet ring cells found in "normal" mucosa

Residual stomach after PG carries persistent cancer risk

### 4.1.4 Metastatic Disease (M1)

Definition: Distant metastases to liver, lung, peritoneum (including positive cytology), distant lymph nodes (para-aortic, mediastinal), or other organs.

Why This Is Absolute:

M1 disease is incurable by surgery. Median survival with chemotherapy is 10-14 months [246]. Gastrectomy provides no survival benefit and delays systemic therapy.

REGATTA Trial Evidence [171]:

Randomized trial (n=175) of palliative gastrectomy + chemotherapy vs. chemotherapy alone for incurable gastric cancer:

Primary endpoint (OS): 16.6 months (gastrectomy) vs. 14.3 months (chemo alone), HR 1.09, p=0.70 (NO BENEFIT)

Complications: 43% (gastrectomy) vs. 8% (chemo alone), p<0.001

Time to chemotherapy: 8.4 weeks (gastrectomy) vs. 0.8 weeks (chemo alone)

Conclusion: Gastrectomy in M1 disease harms patients—delays effective therapy, adds morbidity, provides no survival benefit.

The "Oligometastatic" Controversy:

Scenario: Single 1.8 cm liver metastasis in segment 6, otherwise resectable proximal gastric cancer.

Argument: "This is oligometastatic disease—synchronous gastrectomy + hepatic metastasectomy may be curative."

Counterargument:

No randomized trial supports this approach

Retrospective series show 5-year survival 15-28% [247]—better than M1 chemotherapy alone (5%) but likely reflects selection bias (biologically favorable tumors)

Most patients (70-80%) develop recurrence within 12 months [248]—suggesting surgery fails to achieve cure

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy should be first-line—if metastases respond, then consider surgery; if progress, surgery would have been futile

For PG-DTR: Any M1 disease is absolute contraindication. If pursuing aggressive approach for oligometastatic disease, TG (not PG-DTR) is required—ensures comprehensive nodal clearance.

Positive Peritoneal Cytology (CY1):

Free-floating malignant cells in peritoneal lavage fluid = stage IVB disease (median survival 10.5 months) [249].

Management:

CY1 is absolute contraindication to any curative gastrectomy

Proceed with palliative chemotherapy

Consider feeding jejunostomy if symptomatic obstruction anticipated

Do not perform gastrectomy "to debulk disease"—cytoreduction has no role in gastric cancer (unlike ovarian cancer).

### 4.1.5 Suspicious Nodes in Stations 4d, 5, or 6

Already discussed in Chapter 3.4.2, but warrants emphasis:

ANY lymph node in these stations that meets suspicious criteria (≥8 mm, round, heterogeneous, or clustering) is absolute contraindication to PG-DTR—even if all other criteria are perfect.

Rationale: These stations are not resected in PG-DTR. Leaving metastatic nodes guarantees locoregional recurrence.

Management:

If node truly suspicious: EUS-FNA confirmation → if positive, stage as cN+ → TG required

If node borderline (7-8 mm, oval but slightly hypoechoic): Options include:

Diagnostic laparoscopy with frozen section biopsy of node

Proceed with TG (conservative approach)

Proceed with PG-DTR but obtain intraoperative frozen section—if positive, convert to TG

Never rationalize: "I'll remove the node separately and if positive, do completion gastrectomy later." Staged resections have higher morbidity and worse oncological outcomes than single definitive operation [250].

### 4.2 Relative Contraindications: Case-by-Case Decision Making

Relative contraindications represent situations where PG-DTR may be feasible but carries elevated risk. These require individualized assessment—weighing functional benefits against oncological uncertainty.

### 4.2.1 Tumor Size 5-6 cm

Evidence: KLASS-05 used 4 cm limit, later expanded to 5 cm by most centers. But what about 5.5 cm or 6 cm tumors?

Risk Analysis by Size [251]:

| Size Category | N+ Rate (cT1) | Station 4d/5/6 Involvement | 5-Year RFS After PG-DTR |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≤3 cm | 6.2% | 0.4% | 96.8% |
| 3.1-4 cm | 8.4% | 0.8% | 94.2% |
| 4.1-5 cm | 11.2% | 1.4% | 91.4% |
| 5.1-6 cm | 14.8% | 2.8% | 86.2% |
| >6 cm | 22.4% | 6.4% | 74.8% |

Interpretation:

5.1-6 cm tumors: Nodal involvement nearly doubles vs. ≤5 cm (14.8% vs. 11.2%)

Station 4d/5/6 involvement doubles (2.8% vs. 1.4%)—still low absolute rate but trend concerning

5-year RFS drops to 86%—acceptable but lower than 94% for smaller tumors

Decision Framework:

Favorable Features Allow Accepting 5-6 cm Size:

All other criteria perfect (cT1aN0, differentiated, distance from GEJ >3 cm, no concerning nodes)

Patient strongly motivated for function preservation

Surgeon experienced (>50 PG-DTR cases)

Institutional capability for frozen section of stations 4d/5/6 intraoperatively

Unfavorable Features Make 5-6 cm Unacceptable:

T1b-SM3 (deep submucosal invasion)

Moderately differentiated (vs. well-differentiated)

Any borderline node anywhere (even station 3)

Patient elderly with limited life expectancy (functional benefit less impactful)

Practical Guideline:

5.0-5.5 cm: Acceptable with informed consent about being at upper limit

5.5-6.0 cm: Multidisciplinary discussion required, lean toward TG

>6.0 cm: Contraindication to PG-DTR

### 4.2.2 Distance from GEJ 1-2 cm

KLASS-05 required ≥2 cm [18]. But 1.8 cm tumors present—are they automatically excluded?

Surgical-Technical Consideration:

Proximal Margin Requirement: ≥1 cm microscopically (ideally 2 cm) to ensure R0 resection [252].

Geometric Constraint:

Tumor at 1.8 cm from GEJ

Need 2 cm proximal margin → resection at -0.2 cm (i.e., into esophagus)

Problem: Esophageal resection required, complicates PG-DTR

Alternative Approach: Accept 1 cm proximal margin (vs. ideal 2 cm).

Evidence for 1 cm Margin Adequacy [253]:

Study of 1,482 proximal gastrectomies with variable margin:

| Proximal Margin | R0 Resection Rate | Local Recurrence | 5-Year OS |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ≥2 cm | 98.4% | 2.1% | 92.8% |
| 1-2 cm | 96.2% | 3.8% | 91.2% |
| <1 cm | 88.6% | 12.4% | 78.4% |

Interpretation:

1-2 cm margin: Acceptable (R0 96%, local recurrence 3.8%)

<1 cm margin: Unacceptable (R0 only 89%, local recurrence 12%)

Decision Framework for 1-2 cm from GEJ:

Acceptable:

Tumor 1.5-2 cm from GEJ

Small tumor (≤3 cm)—easier to achieve margin

Non-ulcerated—clear endoscopic demarcation

Frozen section capability—confirm negative margin intraoperatively

Unacceptable:

Tumor <1.5 cm from GEJ

Large tumor (>4 cm)—difficult to preserve esophageal length

Ulcerated/infiltrative—unclear endoscopic borders

Siewert type II tumor (truly GEJ, not gastric)—requires esophagectomy approach

Intraoperative Strategy:

Mobilize distal esophagus extensively (into mediastinum if needed)

Transect stomach 1.5-2 cm proximal to tumor

### Frozen section of proximal margin MANDATORY

If positive → extend resection until negative (may require esophagectomy)

If cannot achieve negative margin without esophagectomy → abort PG-DTR, perform esophagogastrectomy (different operation entirely)

### 4.2.3 Age >75-80 Years

KLASS-05 capped enrollment at age 80 [18], but physiological age ≠ chronological age.

Competing Considerations:

Pro-PG-DTR in Elderly:

Elderly most vulnerable to TG morbidity (malnutrition, sarcopenia, frailty)

Limited life expectancy makes long-term oncological outcomes less relevant (10-year survival depends more on comorbidity than cancer)

Quality over quantity—preserving ability to eat orally is paramount

Con-PG-DTR in Elderly:

Operative complexity of PG-DTR (3 anastomoses) vs. TG (1 anastomosis)—is added technical difficulty justified?

Cognitive decline may impair adaptation to altered eating patterns

Frailty increases perioperative risk regardless of technique

Evidence: Octogenarian Outcomes [254]:

Propensity-matched analysis (n=168, 84 PG-DTR vs. 84 TG, age ≥80):

| Outcome | PG-DTR | TG | p-value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 30-day mortality | 2.4% | 4.8% | 0.41 (NS) |
| Major complications (≥Grade III) | 11.9% | 16.7% | 0.38 (NS) |
| Hospital stay (days) | 10.2 ± 3.8 | 13.4 ± 5.2 | 0.002 |
| 1-year weight loss | -6.8% | -17.2% | <0.001 |
| 1-year hemoglobin | 12.2 g/dL | 10.4 g/dL | <0.001 |
| 3-year survival | 84.2% | 81.8% | 0.68 (NS) |

Interpretation: PG-DTR provides functional benefit without increased perioperative risk in carefully selected octogenarians.

Selection Criteria for Elderly PG-DTR:

Required:

ECOG 0-1 (independent in ADLs)

Charlson Comorbidity Index ≤3

No severe frailty (Edmonton Frail Scale <8)

Adequate cardiopulmonary reserve (METS >4, LVEF >40%, FEV1 >60% predicted)

Proceed with PG-DTR if:

Age 75-85 + meets above criteria + tumor criteria perfect (cT1N0, ≤4 cm, differentiated)

Patient has good life expectancy (projected >5 years based on comorbidity)

Patient values oral intake quality (motivated for function preservation)

Proceed with TG if:

Age >85 (even if physiologically fit—operative duration matters)

Significant frailty or dementia (cannot adapt to PG-DTR eating requirements)

Patient priorities focus on oncological certainty over function (some elderly prefer "maximum surgery" for peace of mind)

### 4.2.4 Prior Abdominal Surgery / Adhesions

Scenario: History of prior laparotomy (appendectomy, cholecystectomy, gynecological surgery, etc.) creates intra-abdominal adhesions.

Impact on PG-DTR:

Technical Challenges:

Adhesiolysis prolongs operative time (15-45 minutes)

Adhesions obscure tissue planes—increases bleeding risk

May obliterate access to celiac/splenic vessels—complicates lymphadenectomy

Bowel injury risk during adhesiolysis (0.5-2%)

Adhesion Severity Stratification:

Minor (No Impact on PG-DTR):

Prior laparoscopic cholecystectomy (adhesions typically in right upper quadrant, remote from gastric operative field)

Prior appendectomy (right lower quadrant adhesions)

Prior cesarean section (lower abdomen adhesions)

Moderate (Proceed with Caution):

Prior open cholecystectomy (may involve gallbladder fossa adhesions to stomach/duodenum)

Prior splenectomy (left upper quadrant adhesions, splenic hilum obliterated—complicates station 10 assessment)

Prior partial gastrectomy for ulcer disease (distorts anatomy)

Severe (Relative Contraindication):

Prior gastric surgery (Billroth, gastrojejunostomy)—anatomy dramatically altered

Prior pancreatic surgery (Whipple, distal pancreatectomy)—obscures celiac/SMA anatomy

History of peritonitis with diffuse adhesions

Decision Framework:

Proceed with Laparoscopic PG-DTR:

Minor adhesions

Experienced surgeon (>50 laparoscopic gastrectomies)

Low threshold to convert to open if adhesiolysis unsafe

Proceed with Open PG-DTR:

Moderate-severe adhesions

Better visualization, safer adhesiolysis

Similar oncological outcomes to laparoscopic [255]

Proceed with TG Instead:

Severe adhesions + borderline tumor criteria (e.g., 4.8 cm tumor + prior gastric surgery)—risk/benefit favors oncological simplicity of TG

### 4.2.5 Multifocal Disease

Definition: ≥2 separate adenocarcinomas in stomach.

Incidence: 4-8% of gastric cancers [256].

Why This Matters for PG-DTR:

Scenario: Dominant 3 cm cT1N0 tumor in cardia + separate 0.8 cm cT1a tumor in distal body.

Challenge:

Proximal lesion meets PG-DTR criteria

Distal lesion requires resection—but is located in area that would be preserved with PG

Options:

### Option A: Total Gastrectomy

Resects both lesions definitively

Oncologically safest

Eliminates future risk of additional primaries in residual stomach

### Option B: Proximal Gastrectomy + Endoscopic Resection

PG-DTR for dominant proximal lesion

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) for small distal lesion (if T1a, <2 cm, differentiated)

Preserves function

Risk: ESD may be incomplete; distal lesion may recur

### Option C: Extended Proximal Gastrectomy

Resect proximal tumor with PG technique

Extend distal resection margin to encompass distal tumor (leaves only small antral/pyloric remnant)

Technically complex; minimal residual stomach defeats purpose of PG-DTR

Evidence: No comparative data exist. Multifocal disease was excluded from KLASS-05 [18].

Practical Guideline:

If distal lesion is T1a, <1.5 cm, well-differentiated: Consider PG-DTR + ESD (accept risk of ESD recurrence, salvageable with completion gastrectomy)

If distal lesion is T1b, >1.5 cm, or poorly differentiated: Proceed with TG (attempting PG-DTR leaves oncological uncertainty)
